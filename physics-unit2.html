2.1 Periodic and Oscillatory Motion

2.1.1 Periodic Motion

Definition: A motion is periodic when the motion repeats itself after equal intervals of time.

The minimum time after which the motion repeats is called the time period T. The number of complete repetitions per second is the frequency f.

f = 1/T     and     T = 1/f

Examples include the rotation of a wheel at constant speed, revolution of a planet and the regular vibration of a tuning fork. A motion may be periodic without being a straight-line oscillation.

2.1.2 Oscillatory Motion

Definition: Oscillatory motion is a repeated to-and-fro motion of a body about a fixed equilibrium or mean position.

A simple oscillating system has a mean position and two extreme positions. During an oscillation the body moves from one extreme to the other and returns to the original extreme.

EXTREME MEAN EXTREME A ------------------ O ------------------ B ← equilibrium → body oscillates about O At O: displacement is zero. At A/B: magnitude of displacement is maximum.

2.1.4 Relation between Frequency and Time Period

If one oscillation takes T seconds, then in one second the number of oscillations is 1/T. Therefore:

f = 1/T
Example: A particle completes 20 oscillations in 5 s. Frequency = 20/5 = 4 Hz. Therefore T = 1/4 = 0.25 s.

2.2 Simple Harmonic Motion

2.2.1 Meaning of SHM

Definition: Simple harmonic motion is an oscillatory motion in which the restoring acceleration is directly proportional to the displacement from the mean position and is directed towards the mean position.
a ∝ −x    or    a = −ω²x

The negative sign is important. It shows that acceleration is opposite to displacement and therefore points towards the equilibrium position.

2.2.2 Why the Negative Sign Appears

Suppose the particle is displaced to the right of the mean position. Its restoring force must act towards the left. If displacement is taken positive to the right, acceleration is negative. Similarly, when the particle is on the left, displacement is negative and acceleration becomes positive.

RESTORING ACCELERATION ← a A -------- P -------- O displacement → x At P on right: x positive, a negative. At P on left: x negative, a positive. At O: x = 0 and a = 0.

2.2.3 Equation of SHM

A convenient displacement equation is:

x = A sin(ωt + φ)

Here A is amplitude, ω is angular frequency, t is time and φ is the initial phase. An equivalent cosine form may also be used depending on the chosen initial condition.

ω = 2πf = 2π/T

2.2.4 Velocity in SHM

Starting with x = A sin(ωt + φ), differentiation with respect to time gives:

v = dx/dt = Aω cos(ωt + φ)

Using sin²θ + cos²θ = 1, the velocity-displacement relation becomes:

v² = ω²(A² − x²)

Thus velocity is maximum at the mean position and becomes zero at the extreme positions.

vmax = ωA

2.2.5 Acceleration in SHM

a = dv/dt = −ω²x

At the mean position x=0, acceleration is zero. At an extreme position x=±A, the magnitude of acceleration is maximum.

amax = ω²A

2.2.6 Force in SHM

From Newton's second law:

F = ma = −mω²x

Therefore the restoring force is proportional to displacement and opposite in direction.

2.2.7 Energy in SHM

For an ideal SHM system, total mechanical energy remains constant when there is no damping.

Total energy E = 1/2 mω²A²

Potential energy at displacement x is:

U = 1/2 mω²x²

Kinetic energy is:

K = 1/2 mω²(A² − x²)

At the mean position, kinetic energy is maximum and potential energy is minimum. At an extreme position, kinetic energy becomes zero and potential energy is maximum.

2.2.8 Phase in SHM

For x=A sin(ωt+φ), the quantity (ωt+φ) is the phase. It tells the instantaneous state of the oscillator. Two oscillations can have the same amplitude and frequency but different phases.

Mean0Maximum0

2.2.9 Examples of SHM

For small angular displacement, the motion of a simple pendulum is approximately SHM. The vibration of a mass attached to a spring is a standard mechanical model of SHM. Molecular vibrations can also be approximated as harmonic near an equilibrium configuration.

2.3 Wave Motion

2.3.1 What is a Wave?

Definition: A wave is a disturbance that transfers energy and information from one region to another without requiring the bulk transport of the medium with the wave.

In a mechanical wave, particles of the medium oscillate about their equilibrium positions while the disturbance travels through the medium.

TRANSVERSE WAVE crest crest / / / \ / \ --------/----\----------------/----\------ mean line / \ / \ / \ / \ trough trough Distance between successive crests = wavelength λ

2.3.2 Mechanical and Electromagnetic Waves

2.3.3 Transverse Waves

Definition: In a transverse wave, particles of the medium vibrate perpendicular to the direction of propagation.

Examples include waves on a stretched string and idealized surface ripples.

2.3.4 Longitudinal Waves

Definition: In a longitudinal wave, particles of the medium vibrate parallel to the direction of propagation.

Sound in air is a common example. The wave consists of alternating compressions and rarefactions.

LONGITUDINAL WAVE Propagation → |||||| | | | |||||| | | | |||||| Compression Rarefaction Compression Rarefaction Particles vibrate back and forth parallel to propagation.

2.3.5 Wavelength

Definition: Wavelength λ is the shortest distance between two successive points of a wave that are in the same phase.

For a transverse wave it can be measured from crest to crest or trough to trough. For a longitudinal wave it is the distance between successive compressions or successive rarefactions.

2.3.6 Frequency and Time Period

Frequency is the number of complete waves or cycles passing a fixed point per second. Its SI unit is hertz (Hz). Time period is the time for one complete cycle.

f = 1/T

2.3.7 Wave Velocity

If a wave travels one wavelength λ in one time period T, its speed is λ/T. Since f=1/T:

v = λ/T = fλ
Example: A wave has frequency 50 Hz and wavelength 2 m. Its speed is v=fλ=50×2=100 m/s.

2.3.8 Amplitude

Amplitude is the maximum displacement of a vibrating particle from its mean position. For an ideal sinusoidal wave, greater amplitude corresponds to greater energy transported by the wave.

2.3.9 Phase Difference

Phase difference tells how far two oscillations are shifted relative to each other. A complete cycle corresponds to 2π radians or 360°.

Phase difference Δφ = 2π(Δx/λ) = 2π(Δt/T)

2.3.10 Wave Equation

A simple travelling wave can be represented as:

y = A sin(kx − ωt + φ)

where k is the wave number:

k = 2π/λ     and     ω = 2πf

The sign before ωt indicates the direction of propagation for the chosen convention. A plus sign represents the opposite direction when the same coordinate convention is used.

2.4 Superposition and Combination of Waves

2.4.1 Principle of Superposition

Principle: When two or more waves overlap in a linear medium, the resultant displacement at any point is the algebraic sum of the displacements produced by the individual waves.
y = y₁ + y₂ + y₃ + ...

The waves can pass through one another and continue their propagation. During overlap, their displacements combine according to the superposition principle.

2.4.2 Constructive Interference

When waves meet in phase, their displacements reinforce one another and the resultant amplitude becomes larger.

Path difference = nλ    (constructive condition)

2.4.3 Destructive Interference

When waves meet with opposite phase, their displacements oppose one another and the resultant amplitude is reduced.

Path difference = (2n+1)λ/2    (destructive condition)
SUPERPOSITION IDEA Wave 1: / / /\ Wave 2: / / /\ Result: /\ /\ /\\ In-phase waves → larger resultant amplitude.

2.4.4 Resultant Amplitude for Two Equal-Frequency Waves

For two waves of amplitudes A₁ and A₂ having phase difference δ:

A = √(A₁² + A₂² + 2A₁A₂ cosδ)

For equal amplitudes A₁=A₂=A₀:

A = 2A₀ |cos(δ/2)|

Therefore δ=0 gives maximum amplitude 2A₀, while δ=π gives zero resultant amplitude for equal waves.

Worked Numericals

Numerical 1 — Frequency and Time Period

Question: An oscillator completes 25 oscillations in 5 s. Find f and T.

f=25/5=5 Hz.

T=1/f=1/5=0.2 s

Answer: f=5 Hz, T=0.2 s.

Numerical 2 — Maximum Velocity

Question: An SHM particle has amplitude 0.04 m and angular frequency 10 rad/s. Find maximum velocity.

vmax=ωA=10×0.04=0.4 m/s

Answer: 0.4 m/s.

Numerical 3 — Maximum Acceleration

Question: For A=0.02 m and ω=5 rad/s, find maximum acceleration.

amax=ω²A=25×0.02=0.5 m/s²

Answer: 0.5 m/s².

Numerical 4 — Wave Speed

Question: A wave has f=100 Hz and λ=1.5 m.

v=fλ=100×1.5=150 m/s

Answer: 150 m/s.

Numerical 5 — Phase Difference

Question: Two points on a wave are separated by λ/4. Find phase difference.

Δφ=2π(λ/4λ)=π/2 rad=90°

Answer: π/2 rad or 90°.

Common Mistakes

  • Writing a=+ω²x for SHM without considering the restoring direction.
  • Confusing amplitude with total peak-to-peak displacement; peak-to-peak value is 2A.
  • Using f=T instead of f=1/T.
  • Forgetting that wavelength is a distance while frequency is cycles per second.
  • Using transverse-wave language for longitudinal compression and rarefaction.
  • Ignoring the phase term when comparing two oscillations.
  • Forgetting that mechanical waves require a material medium.
  • Assuming every periodic motion is necessarily SHM.

Quick Revision

  • Periodic motion repeats after equal time intervals.
  • Oscillatory motion is to-and-fro motion about a mean position.
  • SHM obeys a=−ω²x.
  • x=A sin(ωt+φ).
  • vmax=ωA and amax=ω²A.
  • Frequency f=1/T and angular frequency ω=2πf.
  • Wave speed v=fλ.
  • Transverse vibration is perpendicular; longitudinal vibration is parallel to propagation.
  • Superposition gives resultant displacement equal to the sum of individual displacements.
  • In-phase waves reinforce; opposite-phase waves can cancel.
