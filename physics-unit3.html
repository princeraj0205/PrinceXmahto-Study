3.1 Electric Charge and Electrostatic Concepts

3.1.1 Electric Charge

Electric charge is a basic physical property of matter because of which electrical attraction and repulsion occur. Charge is represented by Q or q.

  • SI unit: coulomb (C)
  • Two types: positive and negative
  • Like charges repel; unlike charges attract.
  • Charge is conserved: total charge of an isolated system remains constant.
  • Charge is quantized: Q = ne, where n is an integer and e is elementary charge.
Q = ne     e = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C (magnitude)
Like charges: (+) ←── repulsion ──→ (+) Unlike charges: (+) ─── attraction ───→ (−)

3.1.2 Coulomb’s Law

Coulomb’s law gives the electrostatic force between two point charges. The magnitude of force is directly proportional to the product of charges and inversely proportional to the square of distance between them.

F = (1 / 4πε₀) × |q₁q₂| / r²

In vacuum, 1/(4πε₀) = 9 × 10⁹ N·m²/C² approximately.

  • F = electrostatic force in newton
  • q₁, q₂ = charges in coulomb
  • r = separation in metre
  • ε₀ = permittivity of free space

Direction: along the line joining the two charges.

Worked Example 1

Question: Two charges 2 μC and 3 μC are 0.30 m apart in air. Find the magnitude of force.

Given: q₁ = 2×10⁻⁶ C, q₂ = 3×10⁻⁶ C, r = 0.30 m.

F = 9×10⁹ × (2×10⁻⁶)(3×10⁻⁶)/(0.30)²

F = 0.60 N. Since both charges are positive, the force is repulsive.

3.1.3 Electric Field

The electric field at a point is the region/property in which an electric test charge experiences electric force. Electric field intensity is force per unit positive test charge.

E = F/q₀

For a point charge:

E = (1 / 4πε₀) × Q/r²

SI unit: N/C (also expressible as V/m).

3.1.4 Electric Lines of Force

Electric lines of force are imaginary lines used to represent the direction and relative strength of an electric field.

  • They start from positive charge and terminate on negative charge (or at infinity).
  • The tangent to a field line gives field direction.
  • Field lines never intersect each other.
  • Closer lines represent a stronger field.
  • For a conductor in electrostatic equilibrium, field lines are normal to the surface.
Positive point charge Negative point charge ↑ ↓ ↗ | ↖ ↘ | ↙ ←── (+) ──→ →── (−) ──← ↘ | ↙ ↗ | ↖ Direction of field around + is outward; around − is inward.

3.1.5 Electric Flux

Electric flux measures the amount of electric field passing through a surface.

Φ = E A cosθ

For a uniform field through a flat surface, where θ is the angle between E and the normal to the surface.

  • SI unit: N·m²/C
  • Maximum flux occurs when θ = 0°.
  • Flux is zero when θ = 90°.

3.1.6 Electric Potential

Electric potential at a point is the work done per unit positive test charge in bringing it from infinity to that point without acceleration.

V = W/q

For a point charge:

V = (1 / 4πε₀) × Q/r

SI unit: volt (V) = joule/coulomb.

3.1.7 Potential Difference

Potential difference between two points is the work done per unit charge in moving a test charge between those points.

V_B − V_A = W_AB/q

Unit: N/C or V/mUnit: volt
E = F/qV = W/q

3.1.8 Electric Dipole

An electric dipole consists of two equal and opposite charges separated by a small distance.

separation = d (+q) ●────────────● (−q) ← dipole axis →

Dipole moment is defined as charge multiplied by separation vector magnitude:

p = qd

SI unit: C·m. Its direction is conventionally from negative charge to positive charge.

3.2 Gauss’ Law and Electric Field of Symmetric Charge Distributions

3.2.1 Gauss’ Law

Gauss’ law relates the total electric flux through a closed surface to the net charge enclosed by that surface.

∮ E · dA = Q_enclosed / ε₀

For a highly symmetric charge distribution, Gauss’ law can make electric-field calculations much easier.

How to use Gauss’ law
  1. Identify the symmetry: cylindrical, planar or spherical.
  2. Choose a suitable closed Gaussian surface.
  3. Determine where E has the same magnitude.
  4. Calculate total flux through the surface.
  5. Set flux equal to enclosed charge divided by ε₀.
  6. Solve for E.

3.2.2 Long Straight Charged Conductor

For a long straight uniformly charged conductor, choose a cylindrical Gaussian surface of radius r and length L.

Gaussian cylinder ╭──────────────────╮ │ charged wire │ │ ││ │ ╰──────────────────╯ radius r Field E is radial and has the same magnitude on the curved surface.

If λ is charge per unit length, enclosed charge is λL. Therefore:

E(2πrL) = λL/ε₀ E = λ/(2πε₀r)

3.2.3 Charged Plane Sheet

For an infinite uniformly charged plane sheet with surface charge density σ, a cylindrical pillbox Gaussian surface gives:

E = σ/(2ε₀)

The field is normal to the sheet and, for the ideal infinite sheet, independent of distance from the sheet.

3.2.4 Charged Spherical Shell

For a uniformly charged spherical shell:

  • Outside the shell: field behaves as if the total charge were concentrated at the centre.
  • Inside the shell: electric field is zero for an ideal conducting spherical shell in electrostatic equilibrium.
Outside: E = (1/4πε₀) Q/r² Inside conducting shell: E = 0
Exam focus: Practice the Gaussian-surface idea and remember why symmetry is important. The official syllabus specifically includes field intensity for a straight charged conductor, charged plane sheet and charged sphere.

3.3 Dielectric and Capacitance

3.3.1 Dielectric

A dielectric is an insulating material that becomes polarized when placed in an electric field. Examples include glass, mica, ceramic and many polymers.

When a dielectric is inserted between capacitor plates, polarization changes the effective electric field and can increase capacitance.

3.3.2 Capacitor

A capacitor is a device used to store electric charge and electrical energy. A simple capacitor has two conducting plates separated by an insulating medium.

+ + + + + + + − − − − − − − ┌───────────┐ ┌───────────┐ │ Positive │ │ Negative │ │ plate │ │ plate │ └───────────┘ └───────────┘ ←──── separation d ────→ dielectric/air

3.3.3 Capacitance

Capacitance is the charge stored per unit potential difference.

C = Q/V

SI unit: farad (F).

3.3.4 Parallel-Plate Capacitor

For two large parallel plates of area A separated by distance d, neglecting edge effects:

C = ε₀A/d

With dielectric of relative permittivity εᵣ:

C = εᵣ ε₀ A/d

3.3.5 Factors Affecting Capacitance

FactorEffect
Plate area ACapacitance increases when plate area increases.
Plate separation dCapacitance decreases when separation increases.
Dielectric materialHigher relative permittivity generally gives higher capacitance.

Worked Example 2

Question: A parallel-plate capacitor has A = 0.02 m² and d = 1 mm. Find capacitance in air.

C = ε₀A/d = (8.854×10⁻¹² × 0.02)/(1×10⁻³)

C ≈ 1.77×10⁻¹⁰ F = 177 pF.

3.4 Magnetic Field, Biot-Savart Law and Lorentz Force

3.4.1 Magnetic Field

A magnetic field is the region around a magnet or current-carrying conductor in which magnetic effects can be observed.

Magnetic field is represented by B. SI unit: tesla (T).

3.4.2 Biot-Savart Law

The Biot-Savart law gives the magnetic field contribution due to a small current element.

dB = (μ₀/4π) × (I dl sinθ)/r²
  • I = current
  • dl = small length element
  • r = distance from current element
  • θ = angle between dl and r
  • μ₀ = permeability of free space

3.4.3 Long Straight Current-Carrying Wire

For a long straight wire carrying current I, at distance r:

B = μ₀I/(2πr)
magnetic field lines ↺ ↺ ↺ │ │ I │ ↻ ↻ ↻ straight wire

Direction is obtained using the right-hand thumb rule: thumb in current direction; curled fingers show magnetic-field direction.

3.4.4 Circular Current-Carrying Wire

At the centre of a circular loop of radius R carrying current I:

B = μ₀I/(2R)

For N turns:

B = μ₀NI/(2R)

This is one of the direct expressions relevant to the official TSO 3e.

3.4.5 Lorentz Force

A charged particle moving in electric and magnetic fields experiences Lorentz force:

F = q(E + v × B)

If only the magnetic field is present:

F = q(v × B) |F| = qvB sinθ

For v parallel to B, force is zero. For v perpendicular to B, force is maximum.

3.5 Magnetic Flux and Electromagnetic Induction

3.5.1 Magnetic Flux

Magnetic flux is the amount of magnetic field passing through a surface.

Φ = BA cosθ

SI unit: weber (Wb).

3.5.2 Faraday’s Law of Electromagnetic Induction

Whenever the magnetic flux linked with a circuit changes, an emf is induced in the circuit. The magnitude of induced emf is proportional to the rate of change of flux linkage.

ε = −N dΦ/dt

For average change:

ε_avg = −N(ΔΦ/Δt)

3.5.3 Lenz’s Law

Lenz’s law states that the direction of induced current is such that it opposes the change in magnetic flux responsible for its production.

The negative sign in Faraday’s law represents this opposition.

Magnet approaching coil N →→→ [ COIL ] ↑ flux increases Induced current produces a magnetic effect that opposes the increase in flux.

3.5.4 Self-Induction

When current in a coil changes, the changing magnetic flux produced by that same coil induces emf in the coil itself. This is self-induction.

ε = −L dI/dt

L is self-inductance; SI unit is henry (H).

3.5.5 Mutual Induction

When a changing current in one coil induces emf in a nearby second coil, the phenomenon is mutual induction.

ε₂ = −M dI₁/dt

M is mutual inductance.

3.5.6 Eddy Current

Eddy currents are circulating currents induced inside bulk conducting materials when magnetic flux through them changes.

They can produce heating and energy loss, but they are also useful in electromagnetic braking, induction heating and some measuring instruments.

3.5.7 Motional EMF

When a conductor moves through a magnetic field and cuts magnetic flux, an emf can be induced across it. For a straight conductor of length l moving with speed v perpendicular to a magnetic field B:

ε = Blv

Worked Example 3

Question: A 200-turn coil experiences a flux change of 0.02 Wb to 0.08 Wb in 0.10 s. Find the magnitude of average induced emf.

|ε| = N|ΔΦ|/Δt = 200×0.06/0.10 = 120 V.

3.6 DC and AC Current

3.6.1 Electric Current

Electric current is the rate of flow of electric charge through a cross-section.

I = Q/t

SI unit: ampere (A).

3.6.2 Direct Current (DC)

In DC, current flows in one direction. Its magnitude may be constant or may vary with time while retaining one direction.

DC current I │ ───────────────── │ └──────────────────────→ t

3.6.3 Alternating Current (AC)

In AC, current changes magnitude and reverses direction periodically. A sinusoidal current may be written as:

i = I₀ sin(ωt + φ)

where I₀ is peak current, ω is angular frequency and φ is phase constant.

3.6.4 Peak Value

The maximum magnitude reached by an AC quantity is its peak value, represented by I₀ for current.

3.6.5 Average Value of AC

For a complete symmetrical sinusoidal cycle, positive and negative halves cancel, so the average value over one complete cycle is zero.

For a sinusoidal current over a half-cycle:

I_avg = 2I₀/π ≈ 0.637 I₀

3.6.6 RMS Value

The root mean square (RMS) value of an AC current is the equivalent DC current that produces the same heating effect in a resistor.

I_rms = I₀/√2 ≈ 0.707 I₀

Similarly, for voltage:

V_rms = V₀/√2
QuantitySinusoidal relationImportant point
Peak currentI₀Maximum instantaneous current
Average current2I₀/π (half-cycle)Full-cycle average = 0
RMS currentI₀/√2Heating-effect equivalent

Worked Example 4

Question: A sinusoidal current has peak value 10 A. Find RMS current and half-cycle average current.

Irms = 10/√2 = 7.07 A.

Iavg = 2×10/π = 6.37 A.

Important Concept Connections

  • Charge → Electric field: charge creates an electric field.
  • Electric field → Force: F = qE for a charge in an electric field.
  • Current → Magnetic field: a current-carrying conductor produces a magnetic field.
  • Changing magnetic flux → Induced emf: Faraday’s law.
  • Changing current → Self-induced emf: self-induction.
  • Moving conductor in magnetic field → Motional emf: ε = Blv under the stated perpendicular-motion condition.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  1. Do not confuse electric field E with electric potential V.
  2. Do not forget that Coulomb force varies as 1/r².
  3. In flux, θ is the angle between the field and the normal to the surface.
  4. Electric field is a vector; potential is a scalar.
  5. For Faraday’s law, do not remove the negative sign when explaining direction; it represents Lenz’s law.
  6. Do not use full-cycle average AC = 0 and then incorrectly use it as the half-cycle average.
  7. For circular-wire magnetic field, check whether the question asks for the field at the centre.
  8. In capacitor numericals, convert mm, μF, nF, pF and μC into consistent SI units.
