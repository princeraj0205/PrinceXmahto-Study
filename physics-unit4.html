4.1 Energy Bands and Band Gap

4.1.1 From Individual Atoms to Energy Bands

In an isolated atom, electrons occupy discrete energy levels. When a very large number of atoms come together to form a solid, the interaction between neighbouring atoms causes these closely spaced energy levels to form broad ranges called energy bands.

The two bands most important for electrical conduction are the valence band and the conduction band. The valence band contains electrons associated with bonding, while electrons that reach the conduction band can participate much more freely in electrical conduction.

Energy ↑ │ CONDUCTION BAND │ ┌────────────────────┐ │ │ electrons can │ │ │ contribute to │ │ │ conduction │ │ └────────────────────┘ │ ↑ Eg │ forbidden gap │ ↓ │ ┌────────────────────┐ │ │ VALENCE BAND │ │ └────────────────────┘ └────────────────────────────→

4.1.2 Forbidden Energy Gap

The band gap Eg is the energy separation between the top of the valence band and the bottom of the conduction band. In the ideal band picture, electrons cannot occupy the forbidden energy region between these bands.

Eg = Ec − Ev

Here Ec is the conduction-band edge and Ev is the valence-band edge. The size of this gap strongly affects whether a material behaves as a conductor, semiconductor or insulator.

4.1.3 Conductor, Semiconductor and Insulator

MaterialBand pictureElectrical behaviour
ConductorValence and conduction bands overlap or a band is partially filled.Many mobile charge carriers are available; current flows readily.
SemiconductorSmall forbidden energy gap.Conductivity lies between conductor and insulator and changes strongly with temperature, light and doping.
InsulatorLarge forbidden energy gap.Very few carriers can reach the conduction band under ordinary conditions.

Common elemental semiconductors include silicon and germanium. Semiconductor behaviour is especially useful because conductivity can be deliberately controlled.

4.1.4 Temperature Effect

In a semiconductor, increasing temperature provides thermal energy that can generate more electron-hole pairs, so conductivity generally increases and resistance decreases. This trend is opposite to the usual metallic trend.

4.2 Charge Carriers and Semiconductor Types

4.2.1 Intrinsic Semiconductor

A chemically pure semiconductor is called an intrinsic semiconductor. At thermal equilibrium, thermal energy can create electron-hole pairs. The number of free electrons and holes is equal in an intrinsic semiconductor.

n = p = nᵢ

Here n is electron concentration, p is hole concentration and ni is intrinsic carrier concentration.

INTRINSIC SEMICONDUCTOR Covalent bonding in silicon Si — Si — Si | | | Si — Si — Si Thermal energy can break a bond: Si — Si ↓ electron + hole electron (−) moves through conduction states hole (+ effective carrier) moves through valence states

4.2.2 Hole

When an electron leaves a covalent bond, the empty state behaves as a positive charge carrier called a hole. A hole is not a separate positive particle in the same sense as a proton; it is a useful model for describing the missing electron state in a semiconductor.

4.2.3 Extrinsic Semiconductor

A semiconductor whose conductivity is intentionally changed by adding a small controlled amount of impurity is called an extrinsic semiconductor. This process is called doping.

4.2.4 n-Type Semiconductor

When a suitable pentavalent impurity is added to silicon or germanium, four impurity electrons can participate in covalent bonding while the fifth electron is relatively weakly bound and can become a conduction electron. Such material is called n-type.

n-type: donor impurity → extra conduction electrons → electrons are majority carriers → holes are minority carriers.

4.2.5 p-Type Semiconductor

When a suitable trivalent impurity is added, one bond is short of an electron. This creates a hole. Such material is called p-type.

p-type: acceptor impurity → holes are created → holes are majority carriers → electrons are minority carriers.

4.2.6 Drift Velocity

When an electric field is applied to a semiconductor, charge carriers acquire an average directed velocity superimposed on their random thermal motion. This average directed velocity is called drift velocity.

vd = μE

For electrons, the conventional current direction is opposite to electron drift direction. For holes, hole drift and conventional current are in the same general direction.

4.2.7 Mobility

Mobility measures how readily a charge carrier acquires drift velocity under an applied electric field.

μ = vd/E

Its SI unit is m²/(V·s).

4.2.8 Current Density

Current density is electric current per unit cross-sectional area normal to the direction of current flow.

J = I/A

For carrier conduction, the current density depends on carrier concentration, charge and drift velocity. For one carrier type:

J = nqvd

For both electrons and holes in a semiconductor, their contributions add in the conventional current description:

J = q(nμn + pμp)E

4.2.9 Drift Current

Current caused by the directed motion of charge carriers under an applied electric field is called drift current. It exists because the electric field produces drift motion.

4.2.10 Diffusion Current

When carrier concentration is not uniform, carriers tend to move from a region of higher concentration toward a region of lower concentration. This concentration-gradient-driven current is called diffusion current.

Carrier concentration HIGH n LOW n ● ● ● ● ● ● ● ─────────────→ ● ● diffusion direction No external electric field is required for the basic concentration-gradient process.

Drift and diffusion are distinct mechanisms. Drift is associated with an electric field, whereas diffusion is associated with a non-uniform carrier concentration.

4.2.11 Law of Mass Action

For a semiconductor in thermal equilibrium, the product of electron concentration and hole concentration is related to intrinsic carrier concentration:

np = nᵢ²

For intrinsic material, n=p=ni, which immediately gives np=ni². In an n-type semiconductor, electron concentration increases, so equilibrium hole concentration correspondingly decreases; in p-type material the opposite occurs.

Exam focus: Be able to distinguish intrinsic/extrinsic, n-type/p-type, majority/minority carriers, drift/diffusion and mobility/current density.

4.3 p-n Junction and Its Electrical Behaviour

4.3.1 Formation of a p-n Junction

A p-n junction is formed when p-type and n-type regions exist together in the same semiconductor crystal. Immediately after junction formation, the large carrier concentration difference causes electrons to diffuse from n-side toward p-side and holes to diffuse from p-side toward n-side.

As mobile carriers leave the neighbourhood of the junction, uncovered ionized donor and acceptor atoms remain. This region becomes depleted of mobile carriers and is called the depletion layer.

p-side junction n-side holes →→→ | DEPLETION REGION | ←←← electrons | fixed ions | (-) (-) (-) (+) (+) (+) acceptor donor ions Electric field develops across the depletion region. It opposes further diffusion of majority carriers.

4.3.2 Depletion Layer

The depletion layer is a region near the junction containing very few mobile charge carriers compared with the neutral p and n regions. The fixed charged ions create an internal electric field.

4.3.3 Barrier Potential

The internal electric field creates a potential difference called the barrier potential. It opposes further diffusion of majority carriers across the junction at equilibrium.

The exact numerical barrier potential depends on semiconductor material, temperature and junction conditions; therefore it should not be treated as one universal constant for every diode.

4.3.4 Unbiased Junction

With no external voltage, diffusion and drift processes reach equilibrium. The depletion region and built-in electric field remain present, but the net external current is zero under equilibrium conditions.

4.3.5 Forward Bias

In forward bias, the p-side is connected to the positive terminal and the n-side to the negative terminal of the external source. The applied field opposes the junction barrier, reducing the effective barrier and allowing substantial majority-carrier injection when the applied voltage is sufficient.

FORWARD BIAS + battery − battery │ │ │ p | junction | n │ └───────────●|──────────|●──────────┘ → conventional current Barrier is reduced; depletion width decreases.

4.3.6 Reverse Bias

In reverse bias, the p-side is connected to the negative terminal and the n-side to the positive terminal. The depletion region widens and the barrier effect increases. A small reverse current due to minority carriers exists before breakdown conditions are reached.

REVERSE BIAS − battery + battery │ │ │ p | junction | n │ └───────────●|──────────|●──────────┘ Depletion width increases; conventional current is very small until breakdown region is reached.

4.3.8 Diode as a Rectifying Device

A diode allows current much more readily in one direction than the other. This directional conduction makes it useful for rectification—conversion of an alternating voltage into a unidirectional or pulsating output.

4.3.9 Half-Wave Rectifier

A half-wave rectifier uses a single diode to pass one half-cycle of an AC input while blocking the opposite half-cycle.

AC source ───►|─── Load ─── return Positive half-cycle: diode conducts → output pulse Negative half-cycle: diode blocks → output ≈ 0 Input: ~ ~ ~ ~ ~ Output: /‾\____/‾\____/‾\___

The output is pulsating DC, not smooth constant DC. A filter capacitor can be added in practical power supplies to reduce ripple.

4.3.10 Full-Wave Rectifier

A full-wave rectifier uses both half-cycles of the AC input so that current through the load has the same direction during both halves. Common implementations use a centre-tapped arrangement with two diodes or a bridge arrangement with four diodes.

4.4 Photocells and Solar Cells

4.4.1 Semiconductor Interaction with Light

Light can transfer energy to electrons in a semiconductor. If the photon energy is sufficient, an electron can be excited and an electron-hole pair can be generated. This light-sensitive behaviour is the basis of several photonic semiconductor devices.

4.4.2 Photocell

A photocell is a light-sensitive device in which an electrical quantity changes in response to incident light. Semiconductor photocells are used for light detection and automatic control.

Light intensity changes → semiconductor carrier population/current changes → electrical signal changes → circuit detects or uses the signal.

4.4.3 Engineering Applications of Photocells

  • Automatic street-light control.
  • Object detection and counting.
  • Light meters and sensing systems.
  • Security and alarm systems.
  • Industrial automation and control.

4.4.4 Solar Cell

A solar cell is a semiconductor device that converts incident light energy directly into electrical energy through the photovoltaic effect.

SOLAR CELL — BASIC WORKING sunlight ↓↓↓↓↓ ┌─────────────────┐ │ front contact │ │ p-type / junction│ │ n-type region │ │ back contact │ └─────────────────┘ │ │ └─load┘ Photons create charge carriers. The junction electric field separates carriers. External circuit receives electrical power.

4.4.5 Working Principle

  1. Sunlight falls on the semiconductor junction.
  2. Photons with sufficient energy generate electron-hole pairs.
  3. The built-in electric field of the junction helps separate the carriers.
  4. The separated charges create a potential difference.
  5. When an external load is connected, current flows through the circuit and electrical power is delivered.

4.4.6 Photocell vs Solar Cell

PhotocellSolar cell
Primarily used to detect or respond to light.Primarily designed to convert light energy into useful electrical power.
Output may be used as a control signal.Output is used to supply an electrical load or charge a storage system.

4.4.7 Solar Cell Applications

  • Solar panels for electricity generation.
  • Calculators and low-power electronics.
  • Remote sensors and monitoring stations.
  • Satellite and space power systems.
  • Off-grid lighting and rural power systems.

Worked Examples

Example 1 — Mobility

Question: A carrier has drift velocity 2×10⁻³ m/s under an electric field of 100 V/m. Find mobility.

Use μ=vd/E.

μ = (2×10⁻³)/100 = 2×10⁻⁵ m²/(V·s)

Answer: 2×10⁻⁵ m²/(V·s).

Example 2 — Current Density

Question: A current of 2 A flows uniformly through an area of 4×10⁻⁴ m². Find current density.
J = I/A = 2/(4×10⁻⁴) = 5×10³ A/m²

Answer: 5×10³ A/m².

Example 3 — Law of Mass Action

Question: At thermal equilibrium, a semiconductor has electron concentration n=10¹⁶ m⁻³ and intrinsic concentration ni=10¹³ m⁻³. Find hole concentration.
np = ni² → p = ni²/n = (10¹³)²/10¹⁶ = 10¹⁰ m⁻³

Answer: 10¹⁰ m⁻³.

Common Mistakes

  1. Do not say a p-type semiconductor is negatively charged overall. Doping does not make the bulk material electrically charged.
  2. Do not confuse a hole with a proton; a hole represents a missing electron state.
  3. Drift current is field-driven; diffusion current is concentration-gradient-driven.
  4. Do not confuse depletion region with a region having more mobile carriers; it is depleted of mobile carriers.
  5. Forward bias and reverse bias connections must be drawn correctly.
  6. A rectifier output is pulsating DC unless filtering is applied.
  7. Photocell detection and solar-cell power generation are related but not identical functions.
  8. Keep majority and minority carrier definitions clear.
