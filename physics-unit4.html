4.1 Energy Bands and Band Gap

4.1.1 Why Energy Bands Are Needed

In an isolated atom, electrons occupy discrete energy levels. When a very large number of atoms come together to form a solid, these energy levels become very closely spaced and form energy bands.

The two important bands for electrical conduction are the valence band and conduction band.

4.1.2 Valence Band

The valence band is the highest energy band that is normally occupied by valence electrons at low temperature. Electrons in this band are associated with atoms and normally do not contribute freely to conduction unless they receive sufficient energy.

4.1.3 Conduction Band

The conduction band is an allowed energy band in which electrons can move through the material and contribute to electrical conduction.

4.1.4 Forbidden Energy Gap / Band Gap

The energy region between the valence band and conduction band in which electron energy states are not allowed is called the forbidden energy gap or band gap.

Eg = Ec − Ev

where Eg is band-gap energy, Ec is conduction-band edge and Ev is valence-band edge.

4.1.5 Conductor

In a conductor, the energy bands permit electrons to move easily. The effective energy gap between relevant bands is very small or bands can overlap. Therefore conductors generally have high electrical conductivity.

Examples: copper, aluminium, silver.

4.1.6 Semiconductor

A semiconductor has electrical conductivity between that of a good conductor and an insulator. It has a relatively small band gap, so some electrons can acquire enough energy to move into the conduction band.

Examples: silicon (Si), germanium (Ge).

4.1.7 Insulator

An insulator has a large forbidden energy gap, making it difficult for electrons to move into the conduction band under ordinary conditions.

Examples: glass, rubber, many ceramics.

4.2 Intrinsic and Extrinsic Semiconductors

4.2.1 Intrinsic Semiconductor

A pure semiconductor without intentional impurity addition is called an intrinsic semiconductor.

In an intrinsic semiconductor, thermal energy can create electron-hole pairs. The number of free electrons is equal to the number of holes.

n = p = nᵢ

Here n = electron concentration, p = hole concentration and nᵢ = intrinsic carrier concentration.

Pure semiconductor atom structure (conceptual) covalent bonds •────────•────────• │ │ │ •────────•────────• Thermal energy can break a bond: electron → conduction band hole → left behind in valence band

4.2.2 Hole

A hole is the vacancy created when an electron leaves a covalent bond. The hole behaves as an effective positive charge carrier because neighbouring electrons can move to fill the vacancy, making the vacancy appear to move in the opposite direction.

4.2.3 Extrinsic Semiconductor

A semiconductor whose electrical conductivity is intentionally increased by adding a small, controlled amount of impurity is called an extrinsic semiconductor. This process is called doping.

There are two main types: n-type and p-type.

4.2.4 n-Type Semiconductor

When a suitable pentavalent impurity is added to a pure semiconductor, extra electrons become available for conduction. Such material is called n-type semiconductor.

The impurity contributes extra electrons and is commonly called a donor impurity.

Majority carriers: electrons. Minority carriers: holes.

4.2.5 p-Type Semiconductor

When a suitable trivalent impurity is added, vacancies/holes are created in the bonding structure. Such material is called p-type semiconductor.

The impurity is commonly called an acceptor impurity.

Majority carriers: holes. Minority carriers: electrons.

4.2.6 Drift Velocity

In a semiconductor, charge carriers have random thermal motion. When an external electric field is applied, they acquire a small average directed velocity called drift velocity.

vd = average directed velocity produced by an applied electric field

For simple carrier transport:

vd ∝ E

where E is electric field intensity.

4.2.7 Mobility

Mobility measures how easily a charge carrier moves through a material under an electric field.

μ = vd/E

SI unit: m²/(V·s).

4.2.8 Drift Current

Drift current is the current caused by the directed motion of charge carriers under an externally applied electric field.

In an n-type semiconductor, electrons contribute strongly to drift current. In a p-type semiconductor, holes contribute strongly.

4.2.9 Diffusion Current

Diffusion current occurs when carrier concentration is not uniform. Charge carriers naturally move from a region of higher concentration toward a region of lower concentration.

4.2.10 Current Density

Current density is current flowing per unit cross-sectional area normal to the direction of current.

J = I/A

SI unit: A/m².

4.2.11 Law of Mass Action

For a semiconductor in thermal equilibrium, the product of electron concentration and hole concentration is related to the square of intrinsic carrier concentration:

np = nᵢ²

This relation is called the law of mass action.

For intrinsic semiconductor, n = p = nᵢ, so:

np = nᵢ × nᵢ = nᵢ²
Exam point: Learn the differences among intrinsic, n-type and p-type semiconductors, plus drift velocity, mobility, drift/diffusion current, current density and np = nᵢ².

4.3 Depletion Layer, Barrier Potential and p-n Junction

4.3.1 What Is a p-n Junction?

A p-n junction is formed when p-type and n-type semiconductor regions are joined within the same semiconductor material.

p-region junction n-region holes ● ● ● ● ││ ● ● ● electrons ● ● ● ● ● ││ ● ● ● ││ depletion region (very few mobile carriers)

4.3.2 Formation of Depletion Layer

Immediately after the p-type and n-type regions are joined, majority carriers diffuse across the junction because of the concentration difference.

  • Electrons from the n-side diffuse toward the p-side.
  • Holes from the p-side diffuse toward the n-side.
  • Near the junction, electrons and holes recombine.
  • This leaves behind fixed ionized impurity charges.
  • The region becomes depleted of mobile majority carriers.

This region is called the depletion layer or depletion region.

4.3.3 Barrier Potential

The fixed charges in the depletion region create an internal electric field. This produces a potential barrier that opposes further diffusion of majority carriers. This is called the barrier potential.

4.3.4 Forward Bias

In forward bias, the p-side is connected to the positive terminal and the n-side to the negative terminal of the external source.

Forward bias (+ battery) ─── P | JUNCTION | N ─── (− battery) External field reduces the junction barrier → depletion region becomes narrower → majority carriers can cross more easily → current increases strongly after the characteristic knee/threshold region.

4.3.5 Reverse Bias

In reverse bias, the p-side is connected to the negative terminal and the n-side to the positive terminal.

Reverse bias (− battery) ─── P | JUNCTION | N ─── (+ battery) Barrier increases → depletion region widens → majority-carrier current is strongly suppressed → only a small reverse current flows until breakdown conditions are reached.

4.3.6 V-I Characteristics of p-n Junction Diode

The V-I characteristic is a graph showing how diode current changes with applied voltage.

I ↑ │ / │ __/ │ __/ │__________/________________→ V │ forward region │ │─────────────── small reverse current │ \ │ \ breakdown region │

Forward characteristic: current is small at first and then rises rapidly after the characteristic knee/threshold region.

Reverse characteristic: a small reverse current flows; at sufficiently high reverse voltage, breakdown can occur and current increases sharply.

4.3.7 Rectifier

A rectifier is an electronic circuit that converts alternating current (AC) into unidirectional or pulsating direct current (DC).

4.3.8 Half-Wave Rectifier

A half-wave rectifier uses one diode to allow one half-cycle of an AC input to reach the load while blocking the opposite half-cycle.

Working: During the conducting half-cycle the diode is forward biased and current flows through the load. During the opposite half-cycle it is reverse biased and blocks current.

A full-wave rectifier uses both half-cycles of the AC input to produce pulsating DC in the load circuit. Common implementations include centre-tapped and bridge arrangements.

4.4 Photocells and Solar Cells

4.4.1 Photocell

A photocell is a light-sensitive device whose electrical behaviour changes when light falls on it. Semiconductor-based light-sensitive devices are widely used for sensing and control.

4.4.2 Basic Working Principle

When suitable light falls on a semiconductor material, photons can transfer energy to charge carriers. This can change the conductivity or produce a measurable electrical response.

Light ↓↓↓↓↓ ┌───────────────────┐ │ Semiconductor │ │ photo-sensitive │ │ material │ └───────────────────┘ ↓ Change in carriers / current ↓ Electrical signal or switching action

4.4.3 Applications of Photocells

  • Automatic street-light control
  • Light sensing and switching
  • Security and object detection systems
  • Optical control circuits
  • Counting and sensing systems

4.4.4 Solar Cell

A solar cell is a semiconductor device that converts incident light energy into electrical energy by the photovoltaic effect.

4.4.5 Principle of Solar Cell

When light of suitable energy falls on a semiconductor p-n junction, electron-hole pairs can be generated. The built-in electric field of the junction helps separate these carriers, producing a potential difference and allowing current to flow through an external circuit.

Sunlight ↓ ↓ ↓ ↓ ↓ ┌───────────────┐ │ p-n junction│ │ solar cell │ └───────────────┘ │ │ │ │ └─ Load┘ ↓ Electrical power to load

4.4.6 Basic Construction

  • Semiconductor p-n junction
  • Light-receiving surface
  • Electrical contacts
  • Protective layers/covering as required by construction

4.4.7 Working of Solar Cell — Step by Step

  1. Sunlight falls on the semiconductor surface.
  2. Photons transfer energy to the semiconductor.
  3. Electron-hole pairs are generated.
  4. The junction electric field separates the charge carriers.
  5. Charges are collected by the contacts.
  6. When an external load is connected, electrical current flows through the load.

4.4.8 Engineering Applications of Solar Cells

  • Solar power systems
  • Battery charging systems
  • Remote sensors and instruments
  • Satellite and space applications
  • Street lighting and small standalone systems
  • Distributed renewable electricity generation

Numerical / Application Practice

Example 1: If drift velocity is 2×10⁻⁴ m/s under an electric field of 10 V/m, calculate mobility.

μ = vd/E = 2×10⁻⁴/10 = 2×10⁻⁵ m²/(V·s).

Example 2: A conductor carries 2 A current through an area of 4×10⁻⁶ m². Find current density.

J = I/A = 2/(4×10⁻⁶) = 5×10⁵ A/m².

Example 3: At thermal equilibrium, a semiconductor has n = 10¹⁶ m⁻³ and nᵢ = 10¹⁰ m⁻³. Find p.

np = nᵢ² → p = nᵢ²/n = (10¹⁰)²/10¹⁶ = 10⁴ m⁻³.

Common Mistakes

  1. Do not write holes as actual free protons. A hole represents a missing electron in the bonding structure.
  2. Do not confuse majority carrier with only carrier. Minority carriers are also present.
  3. Do not reverse n-type and p-type majority carriers.
  4. Do not confuse drift current with diffusion current.
  5. In forward bias, p is connected to positive and n to negative.
  6. In reverse bias, p is connected to negative and n to positive.
  7. Do not say a rectifier converts AC directly into perfectly constant DC; its output is initially pulsating DC and filtering may be used in practical supplies.
  8. Do not confuse a photocell's light-sensing function with the energy-generation role of a solar cell.
