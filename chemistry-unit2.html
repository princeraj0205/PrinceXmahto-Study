2.1 Introduction, Sources of Water and Hardness

\n\n

2.1.1 Importance of Water

\n
Definition — Water Resource: A water resource is a naturally occurring source from which water can be obtained for domestic, agricultural, industrial or other uses.
\n

Water is required for drinking, cooking, sanitation, agriculture, cooling, steam generation, washing, chemical processing and many industrial operations. The quality required depends on the intended use. For example, ordinary domestic water and high-purity water required in specialized industrial processes are not necessarily of the same quality.

\n\n

2.1.2 Sources of Water

\n
Definition — Source of Water: A source of water is a natural or collected reservoir from which water is obtained for use.
\n\n\n\n\n\n\n
SourceExamplesTypical characteristics
Rain waterRainfall collected directlyGenerally low in dissolved salts, but can absorb gases and atmospheric impurities.
Surface waterRivers, lakes, ponds, reservoirsCan contain suspended matter, microorganisms and dissolved substances.
Ground waterWells, tube wells, springsUsually filtered naturally through soil but may contain dissolved mineral salts.
Sea waterSeas and oceansContains a high concentration of dissolved salts and requires desalination for many uses.
\n\n
Exam point: Do not write that every natural source is automatically safe for drinking. Natural water may contain physical, chemical and biological impurities.
\n\n

2.1.3 Impurities in Natural Water

\n
Definition — Impurity: An impurity is an unwanted substance present in water that can affect its suitability for a particular use.
\n\n\n\n\n\n\n\n
CategoryExamplesPossible effect
Suspended impuritiesSand, clay, siltTurbidity and sediment formation
Dissolved inorganic impuritiesCa²⁺, Mg²⁺, chloride, sulphate etc.Hardness, taste, scaling or other effects
Dissolved gasesO₂, CO₂ etc.Can affect corrosion and water chemistry
Biological impuritiesMicroorganismsMay affect health and sanitation
Organic matterNatural or waste-derived organic substancesCan increase oxygen demand and affect taste/odour
\n\n

2.1.4 Hard Water

\n
Definition — Hard Water: Hard water is water that does not readily form lather with soap because of dissolved salts, especially salts of calcium and magnesium.
\n

In ordinary soap use, Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ ions react with soap and form insoluble substances commonly called soap scum. Therefore more soap is consumed before satisfactory lather appears.

\n\n
\n
\nSOAP + Ca²⁺ / Mg²⁺\n        │\n        ▼\ninsoluble calcium/magnesium soap\n        │\n        ▼\nless lather + soap wastage\n
\n
\n\n

2.1.5 Soft Water

\n
Definition — Soft Water: Soft water is water that forms lather comparatively easily with soap and contains little or no troublesome calcium and magnesium hardness.
\n\n

2.1.6 Temporary Hardness

\n
Definition — Temporary Hardness: Temporary hardness is hardness mainly associated with dissolved bicarbonates of calcium and magnesium and which can be substantially removed by boiling.
\n

Representative compounds are calcium bicarbonate and magnesium bicarbonate.

\n
\n
Ca(HCO₃)₂ → CaCO₃↓ + CO₂↑ + H₂O
\n
Mg(HCO₃)₂ → MgCO₃↓ + CO₂↑ + H₂O
\n

The simplified educational equations show the basic idea: heating converts bicarbonate hardness into less soluble products. In magnesium systems, further reactions can produce Mg(OH)₂ under appropriate conditions.

\n
\n\n

2.1.7 Permanent Hardness

\n
Definition — Permanent Hardness: Permanent hardness is hardness caused mainly by dissolved salts such as calcium and magnesium chlorides and sulphates that are not removed simply by boiling.
\n

Examples include CaSO₄, MgSO₄, CaCl₂ and MgCl₂.

\n\n

2.1.8 Difference Between Temporary and Permanent Hardness

\n\n\n\n\n\n\n
PointTemporary hardnessPermanent hardness
Main associationBicarbonates of Ca and MgCommonly chlorides and sulphates of Ca and Mg
Effect of boilingCan be substantially removedNot removed simply by boiling
Typical engineering concernScale/deposits and soap consumptionScale/deposits and soap consumption
Removal approachBoiling or suitable treatmentSoftening methods such as lime-soda, zeolite or ion exchange
\n\n
5-mark answer: Define hardness → explain temporary hardness with example → explain permanent hardness with example → write two differences → mention removal methods.

2.2 Degree of Hardness, CaCO₃ Equivalent and EDTA Method

\n\n

2.2.1 Degree of Hardness

\n
Definition — Degree of Hardness: The degree of hardness expresses the quantity of hardness-producing substances in water, commonly reported as an equivalent amount of calcium carbonate (CaCO₃).
\n\n

2.2.2 Why CaCO₃ Equivalent Is Used

\n

Different calcium and magnesium salts have different molar masses and chemical effects. To compare their hardness contribution on a common basis, hardness is expressed as an equivalent quantity of CaCO₃.

\n\n
\nGeneral CaCO₃-equivalent relationship:\n
CaCO₃ equivalent = amount of substance × (equivalent mass of CaCO₃ / equivalent mass of the substance)
\n

For a salt producing a hardness ion, the equivalent-mass approach converts its contribution to a common CaCO₃ basis.

\n
\n\n

2.2.3 Equivalent Weight

\n
Definition — Equivalent Weight: Equivalent weight is the mass of a substance that corresponds to one equivalent in a specified chemical reaction or capacity.
\n
\n
Equivalent weight = Molecular mass / n-factor
\n

For CaCO₃ in hardness calculations, the commonly used equivalent mass is 50 mg per milliequivalent on the CaCO₃ basis.

\n
\n\n

2.2.4 Units of Hardness

\n

Hardness may be expressed in several units. For diploma calculations, mg/L (or ppm) as CaCO₃ is especially important.

\n\n\n\n\n\n
UnitMeaning
mg/L as CaCO₃Milligrams of CaCO₃ equivalent per litre of water.
ppm as CaCO₃Parts per million, commonly numerically equivalent to mg/L for dilute aqueous water samples.
meq/LMilliequivalents per litre.
\n\n

2.2.5 EDTA Method — Basic Principle

\n
Definition — EDTA: EDTA (ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid) is a chelating agent that forms stable complexes with metal ions such as Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺.
\n
Definition — Complexometric Titration: A complexometric titration is a titration in which the analyte is measured through formation of a stable complex with a complexing reagent.
\n\n

The syllabus specifically requires quantitative measurement of water hardness by the EDTA method. In the standard teaching procedure, a measured water sample is buffered to the required pH, an indicator is added, and the sample is titrated against standard EDTA solution. The endpoint is observed through the indicator colour change.

\n\n

2.2.6 Principle of EDTA Hardness Determination

\n
\n
\nWater sample containing Ca²⁺ + Mg²⁺\n                 │\n                 ▼\n      buffer + hardness indicator\n                 │\n                 ▼\n        titration with EDTA\n                 │\n                 ▼\n     Ca/Mg ions complexed by EDTA\n                 │\n                 ▼\n           colour endpoint\n
\n
\n\n
\nImportant chemistry idea: EDTA binds metal ions in a stable complex. At the appropriate pH and with the appropriate indicator, the endpoint corresponds to complexation of the hardness-producing ions present in the test portion.\n
\n\n

2.2.7 Reagents Used in the Standard Teaching Procedure

\n\n\n\n\n\n\n
Reagent/materialPurpose
Water sampleSample whose hardness is being determined
Standard EDTA solutionComplexing/titrating reagent
Buffer solutionMaintains the required pH for the titration
Hardness indicator (commonly Eriochrome Black T in standard teaching procedures)Provides the endpoint colour change
\n\n

2.2.8 EDTA Titration — Step-by-Step

\n
    \n
  1. Measure a known volume of the water sample.
    2. \n
  2. Add the specified buffer solution to establish the required pH.
    3. \n
  3. Add the hardness indicator.
    4. \n
  4. Titrate with standard EDTA solution from a burette while mixing properly.
    5. \n
  5. Observe the endpoint colour change specified by the laboratory procedure.
    6. \n
  6. Record the EDTA volume used.
    7. \n
  7. Use the standardization factor or concentration of EDTA to calculate total hardness, reported as CaCO₃ equivalent.
    8. \n
\n\n
\nIllustrative calculation:\n

Suppose a laboratory standardization states that 1 mL of the EDTA solution corresponds to 1 mg of CaCO₃ equivalent, and a 50 mL water sample requires 12 mL EDTA.

\n
CaCO₃ equivalent in sample = 12 mg
\n
Hardness = (12 mg / 50 mL) × 1000 = 240 mg/L as CaCO₃
\n

Answer: 240 mg/L as CaCO₃.

\n

This is an illustrative numerical example; in an actual experiment, use the standardization value supplied by the laboratory.

\n
\n\n

2.2.9 EDTA Method — Advantages and Precautions

\n
    \n
  • Useful for quantitative determination of total hardness.
    • \n
  • Can measure hardness due to calcium and magnesium together.
    • \n
  • Endpoint detection depends on correct pH, indicator condition and titration technique.
    • \n
  • Do not confuse sample volume with EDTA volume.
    • \n
  • Always write the final unit: mg/L as CaCO₃ or the unit requested.
    • \n
\n\n
Likely exam question: “Explain the EDTA method for determination of hardness of water.” Write principle → apparatus/reagents → procedure → endpoint → calculation → result.

2.3 Municipal Supply of Water and Treatment of Water

\n\n

2.3.1 Municipal Water Supply

\n
Definition — Municipal Water Supply: Municipal water supply is the organized collection, treatment, storage and distribution of water to a community through a public water-supply system.
\n\n

2.3.2 General Municipal Water-Treatment Flow

\n
\n
\nSOURCE\n  │\n  ▼\nCollection / Intake\n  │\n  ▼\nScreening\n  │\n  ▼\nAeration (where required)\n  │\n  ▼\nCoagulation + Flocculation\n  │\n  ▼\nSedimentation\n  │\n  ▼\nFiltration\n  │\n  ▼\nDisinfection\n  │\n  ▼\nStorage\n  │\n  ▼\nDistribution\n
\n
\n

The exact plant configuration can vary with raw-water quality and local requirements. The flow above is a conceptual textbook sequence used to understand the major treatment operations.

\n\n

2.3.3 Screening

\n
Definition — Screening: Screening is the physical removal of relatively large floating or suspended objects from raw water by passing it through screens.
\n

It protects pumps, channels and downstream equipment from large debris.

\n\n

2.3.4 Aeration

\n
Definition — Aeration: Aeration is the process of bringing water into intimate contact with air to promote transfer of gases and assist certain oxidation or odour-removal processes.
\n\n

2.3.5 Coagulation

\n
Definition — Coagulation: Coagulation is the destabilization of fine suspended or colloidal particles so that they can combine into larger removable aggregates.
\n\n

2.3.6 Flocculation

\n
Definition — Flocculation: Flocculation is the gentle mixing process that promotes formation and growth of larger flocs from destabilized particles.
\n\n

2.3.7 Sedimentation

\n
Definition — Sedimentation: Sedimentation is the separation of suspended particles from water by allowing them to settle under gravity.
\n\n

2.3.8 Filtration

\n
Definition — Filtration: Filtration is the passage of water through a porous medium to remove remaining suspended particles and some microorganisms and other contaminants.
\n\n

2.3.9 Disinfection

\n
Definition — Disinfection: Disinfection is the process of destroying or inactivating harmful microorganisms in treated water to reduce health risk.
\n\n
Remember the flow: Screen → condition → coagulate/flocculate → settle → filter → disinfect → store → distribute.
\n\n

Water Softening — Soda-Lime Process

\n
Definition — Water Softening: Water softening is the treatment of water to reduce its hardness, especially hardness caused by calcium and magnesium ions.
\n\n
Definition — Soda-Lime Process: The soda-lime process is a chemical softening method in which calculated quantities of lime and soda ash are added to water so that hardness-producing ions are converted into precipitates that can be removed.
\n\n

Principle

\n

Lime is commonly represented by Ca(OH)₂ and soda ash by Na₂CO₃. The treatment converts dissolved hardness species into relatively insoluble CaCO₃ and Mg(OH)₂ precipitates under appropriate conditions.

\n\n
\nRepresentative reactions:\n
Ca(HCO₃)₂ + Ca(OH)₂ → 2CaCO₃↓ + 2H₂O
\n
Mg(HCO₃)₂ + 2Ca(OH)₂ → Mg(OH)₂↓ + 2CaCO₃↓ + 2H₂O
\n
CaSO₄ + Na₂CO₃ → CaCO₃↓ + Na₂SO₄
\n
\n\n
\n
\nHard water + calculated lime + soda ash\n                 │\n                 ▼\n       chemical precipitation\n          ┌──────────────┐\n          │ CaCO₃ ↓      │\n          │ Mg(OH)₂ ↓    │\n          └──────────────┘\n                 │\n                 ▼\n          settling / removal\n                 │\n                 ▼\n          softened water\n
\n
\n\n

Advantages and Limitations

\n\n\n\n\n\n
AdvantagesLimitations
Suitable for large-scale water treatment.Produces sludge that must be handled.
Can treat different forms of hardness with calculated chemical doses.Requires chemical dosing and process control.
Can reduce hardness significantly.Does not automatically mean complete demineralization.
\n\n

Lime-Soda Calculation Concept

\n

The syllabus specifically requires calculation of the amount of lime and soda required for removal of hardness. Such calculations are based on converting each hardness-producing constituent to a common CaCO₃-equivalent basis, applying the required stoichiometric relationships, and then converting equivalents to the mass of the reagent.

\n
\nCore calculation idea:\n
Required reagent = total required equivalents × equivalent weight of reagent
\n

In practical textbook problems, the exact factor depends on the chemical form and purity of the lime/soda specified.

\n
\n\n
\nMini example of the method:\n

If a problem gives hardness contributions in mg/L as CaCO₃, first add the relevant CaCO₃-equivalent contributions. Then identify which hardness components require lime, soda ash, or both. Apply stoichiometric factors and finally account for reagent purity if the question provides it.

\n
\n\n

Zeolite Method

\n
Definition — Zeolite Softening: Zeolite softening is an ion-exchange softening process in which sodium ions associated with a zeolite exchange with calcium and magnesium ions in hard water.
\n\n

Working Principle

\n

In the sodium-zeolite process, hard water passes through a bed of sodium-form zeolite. Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ ions are retained by the zeolite while Na⁺ ions enter the water.

\n\n
\n
2NaZ + Ca²⁺ → CaZ₂ + 2Na⁺
\n
2NaZ + Mg²⁺ → MgZ₂ + 2Na⁺
\n

Here Z represents the zeolite exchange framework.

\n
\n\n

Regeneration

\n
Definition — Regeneration: Regeneration is the restoration of the ion-exchange capacity of a spent exchange material by treatment with a suitable regenerant.
\n

For sodium-zeolite softeners, concentrated sodium chloride solution is commonly used to restore the sodium form of the exchange material.

\n\n
\n
\nHard water\n    │\n    ▼\n┌─────────────────┐\n│ Sodium zeolite  │\n│     bed         │\n└─────────────────┘\n    │\n    ▼\nSoftened water\n\nRegeneration:\nNaCl solution → restores Na-form zeolite\n
\n
\n\n

Advantages and Limitations

\n
    \n
  • Compact and convenient for continuous softening.
    • \n
  • Produces little sludge compared with precipitation softening.
    • \n
  • Requires regeneration when exchange capacity is exhausted.
    • \n
  • It removes hardness ions by exchange; it is not the same as complete demineralization.
    • \n
\n\n

Ion-Exchange Method

\n
Definition — Ion Exchange: Ion exchange is a process in which ions in a solution are reversibly exchanged with ions attached to an insoluble exchange material.
\n

A demineralization system can use a cation exchanger followed by an anion exchanger. The objective is to remove dissolved cations and anions so that highly purified water can be produced.

\n\n
\n
\nRaw water\n   │\n   ▼\nCation exchanger\n   │\n   │  removes Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, Na⁺ etc.\n   ▼\nAcidic water / H⁺ form\n   │\n   ▼\nAnion exchanger\n   │\n   │  removes anions\n   ▼\nHighly demineralized water\n
\n
\n\n

Cation Exchange

\n
\n
2RH + Ca²⁺ → R₂Ca + 2H⁺
\n

R represents the cation-exchange resin.

\n
\n\n

Anion Exchange

\n
\n
R'OH + Cl⁻ → R'Cl + OH⁻
\n

H⁺ and OH⁻ can combine to form water.

\n
\n\n
Comparison: Lime-soda is primarily precipitation softening; sodium-zeolite is a cation-exchange softening process; a cation + anion exchange system can remove a much wider range of dissolved ions and produce demineralized water.
\n\n\n\n\n\n\n\n
FeatureSoda-limeZeoliteIon exchange
Main mechanismPrecipitationIon exchangeIon exchange
Main purposeReduce hardnessReduce Ca/Mg hardnessCan produce demineralized/high-purity water
SludgeYesVery little compared with precipitationVery little process sludge
RegenerationChemical dosing is continuous/batch as designedYesYes

2.4 Water Quality: BOD, COD and Dissolved Oxygen

\n\n
Definition — Water Quality: Water quality describes the physical, chemical and biological characteristics of water that determine its suitability for a particular use.
\n\n

2.4.1 Water Quality Index — Context

\n

The syllabus places Biological Oxygen Demand, Chemical Oxygen Demand and determination of Dissolved Oxygen under “Water Quality Index.” In this chapter these are taught as indicators/measurements used to understand water quality. The syllabus excerpt does not prescribe a particular numerical WQI calculation formula, so no additional official WQI formula is assumed here.

\n\n

2.4.2 Dissolved Oxygen (DO)

\n
Definition — Dissolved Oxygen: Dissolved oxygen is the concentration of molecular oxygen present in water in dissolved form.
\n

DO is important for aquatic ecosystems and is also an important water-quality parameter. Its concentration can be affected by temperature, mixing, atmospheric contact, photosynthesis, respiration and oxidation of substances in water.

\n\n

2.4.3 Determination of Dissolved Oxygen

\n

A classical analytical approach is the iodometric/Winkler principle. The exact laboratory procedure should follow the practical manual used by the institute.

\n
\n
\nWater sample\n    │\n    ▼\nfix dissolved oxygen chemically\n    │\n    ▼\ngenerate iodine equivalent\n    │\n    ▼\ntitrate iodine with standard titrant\n    │\n    ▼\ncalculate DO concentration\n
\n
\n\n
Definition — Winkler Method: The Winkler method is a classical iodometric method for determining dissolved oxygen in water through chemical fixation of oxygen and subsequent titration of the iodine equivalent.
\n\n
Exam caution: In a laboratory answer, use the exact reagents, endpoint and calculation factor specified by your practical manual. Do not mix a theoretical outline with a different laboratory variant.
\n\n

2.4.4 Biological Oxygen Demand (BOD)

\n
Definition — BOD: Biological Oxygen Demand is the amount of dissolved oxygen consumed by microorganisms while biologically decomposing biodegradable organic matter in a water sample under specified test conditions.
\n

A higher BOD generally indicates a greater biodegradable organic load because more oxygen is required by microorganisms for decomposition.

\n\n
\n
\nOrganic matter in water\n        │\n        ▼\nMicroorganisms use oxygen\n        │\n        ▼\nOrganic matter decomposes\n        │\n        ▼\nDO decreases\n        │\n        ▼\nBOD measurement reflects oxygen demand\n
\n
\n\n

2.4.5 BOD Test Concept

\n
    \n
  1. Measure initial dissolved oxygen of a prepared water sample.
    2. \n
  2. Incubate the sample under specified conditions for the prescribed test period.
    3. \n
  3. Measure final dissolved oxygen.
    4. \n
  4. The oxygen depletion, with the required corrections/dilution factors, is used to calculate BOD.
    5. \n
\n\n
\nSimplified conceptual relationship:\n
Oxygen depletion = Initial DO − Final DO
\n

For an actual BOD calculation, dilution and seed/correction factors may apply according to the test method.

\n
\n\n

2.4.6 Chemical Oxygen Demand (COD)

\n
Definition — COD: Chemical Oxygen Demand is the amount of oxygen equivalent required to chemically oxidize oxidizable substances present in a water sample under specified test conditions.
\n

COD is a chemical test and can respond to substances that may not be rapidly biodegraded in a BOD test.

\n\n

2.4.7 Difference Between BOD and COD

\n\n\n\n\n\n\n\n
BasisBODCOD
MeaningOxygen demand associated with biological decomposition under specified conditions.Oxygen equivalent associated with chemical oxidation under specified conditions.
ProcessBiologicalChemical
Test durationGenerally longer than COD testingGenerally faster
UseIndicates biodegradable organic load under test conditionsIndicates chemically oxidizable load under test conditions
RelationshipOften lower than COD for the same sample, but the relationship depends on composition.Often higher than BOD because it can include a broader chemically oxidizable fraction.
\n\n
One-line memory trick: BOD = biological oxygen demand; COD = chemical oxygen demand; DO = oxygen already dissolved in water.

Unit 2 — Solved Numerical Examples

\n\n

Numerical 1 — CaCO₃ Equivalent

\n
\n

Question: A water sample contains 162 mg/L of Ca(HCO₃)₂. Calculate its CaCO₃ equivalent using the equivalent-weight method.

\n

Step 1 — Molecular mass: Ca(HCO₃)₂ ≈ 162 g/mol.

\n

Step 2 — n-factor: For hardness contribution of Ca²⁺, n = 2.

\n

Step 3 — Equivalent mass: 162/2 = 81.

\n

Step 4 — CaCO₃ equivalent:

\n
162 × 50/81 = 100 mg/L as CaCO₃
\n

Answer: 100 mg/L as CaCO₃.

\n
\n\n

Numerical 2 — Total Hardness From Components

\n
\n

Question: Suppose a water sample has hardness contributions of 80 mg/L and 120 mg/L, both already expressed as CaCO₃ equivalents. Find total hardness.

\n
Total hardness = 80 + 120 = 200 mg/L as CaCO₃
\n

Answer: 200 mg/L as CaCO₃.

\n
\n\n

Numerical 3 — EDTA Titration

\n
\n

Question: 50 mL of water requires 12 mL of EDTA. If 1 mL EDTA = 1 mg CaCO₃ equivalent, find hardness.

\n
Hardness = (12 × 1 × 1000) / 50
\n
= 240 mg/L as CaCO₃
\n

Answer: 240 mg/L as CaCO₃.

\n
\n\n

Numerical 4 — BOD Concept

\n
\n

Question: A properly prepared test sample has initial DO of 8.0 mg/L and final DO of 5.5 mg/L after the prescribed incubation. Ignoring dilution/seed corrections for this simplified example, find oxygen depletion.

\n
Oxygen depletion = 8.0 − 5.5 = 2.5 mg/L
\n

Answer: 2.5 mg/L oxygen depletion. In a formal BOD test calculation, apply the prescribed dilution/correction factors.

\n
\n\n

Numerical 5 — EDTA Formula Form

\n
\n
Hardness (mg/L as CaCO₃) = (V × F × 1000) / Vs
\n

where V = EDTA volume used, F = CaCO₃-equivalent mass corresponding to 1 mL EDTA under the given standardization, and Vs = water sample volume in mL.

\n
\n\n
Numerical rule: Always write Given → Formula → Substitution → Calculation → Unit → Final Answer.

TermDefinition
Water ResourceA natural or collected source from which water is obtained for use.
Hard WaterWater that does not readily form lather with soap because of dissolved hardness-producing salts.
Soft WaterWater that forms lather readily and contains little troublesome calcium and magnesium hardness.
Temporary HardnessHardness mainly associated with bicarbonates of calcium and magnesium and substantially removable by boiling.
Permanent HardnessHardness mainly associated with salts such as calcium and magnesium chlorides and sulphates that are not removed simply by boiling.
CaCO₃ EquivalentA common basis on which hardness-producing substances are expressed as an equivalent amount of calcium carbonate.
EDTAA chelating agent used to form stable complexes with metal ions in complexometric titration.
Complexometric TitrationA titration based on formation of a stable complex between the analyte ion and a complexing reagent.
Municipal Water SupplyAn organized public system for collecting, treating, storing and distributing water to a community.
CoagulationDestabilization of fine suspended or colloidal particles so they can form removable aggregates.
FlocculationGentle mixing that promotes formation and growth of flocs.
SedimentationSeparation of suspended particles by settling under gravity.
FiltrationRemoval of particles by passing water through a porous medium.
DisinfectionDestruction or inactivation of harmful microorganisms in water.
Water SofteningReduction of hardness-producing ions in water.
Soda-Lime ProcessChemical softening using lime and soda ash to precipitate hardness-producing species.
Zeolite SofteningSoftening by exchange of Ca²⁺/Mg²⁺ in water with Na⁺ associated with zeolite.
Ion ExchangeReversible exchange of ions between a solution and an insoluble exchange material.
Dissolved OxygenMolecular oxygen present in water in dissolved form.
BODOxygen demand associated with biological decomposition of biodegradable organic matter under specified test conditions.
CODOxygen equivalent associated with chemical oxidation of oxidizable substances under specified test conditions.
Drinking-Water SpecificationPrescribed quality requirements used to assess suitability of water for potable use.

⚡ Quick Revision Sheet

\n
    \n
  • Hard water: does not readily form lather due mainly to Ca²⁺/Mg²⁺ salts.
    • \n
  • Temporary hardness: mainly bicarbonates; can be substantially removed by boiling.
    • \n
  • Permanent hardness: mainly chlorides/sulphates; not removed simply by boiling.
    • \n
  • Hardness unit: commonly mg/L or ppm as CaCO₃.
    • \n
  • EDTA: forms stable complexes with Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺.
    • \n
  • Municipal treatment: screening → coagulation/flocculation → sedimentation → filtration → disinfection, with other conditioning steps as required.
    • \n
  • Lime-soda: precipitation method.
    • \n
  • Zeolite: sodium-ion exchange softening.
    • \n
  • Ion exchange: cation and anion exchange can produce demineralized water.
    • \n
  • DO: oxygen dissolved in water.
    • \n
  • BOD: biological oxygen demand.
    • \n
  • COD: chemical oxygen demand.
    • \n
  • Higher oxygen demand: generally indicates greater oxidizable/organic load under the relevant test conditions.
    • \n
