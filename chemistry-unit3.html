3.1

3.1 Natural Occurrence of Metals — Minerals and Ores

3.1.1 Metals in Nature

Definition — Natural Occurrence of Metals: Natural occurrence of metals refers to the forms and combinations in which metallic elements are found in the Earth's crust.

Some metals occur in a relatively free state, while many occur chemically combined with other elements. Extraction is therefore designed according to the chemical and physical nature of the material containing the metal.

3.1.2 Mineral

Definition — Mineral: A mineral is a naturally occurring inorganic substance or material having a characteristic chemical composition and structure. In metallurgy, minerals containing useful metallic compounds may serve as sources of metals.

3.1.3 Ore

Definition — Ore: An ore is a naturally occurring mineral or mineral aggregate from which a metal can be extracted conveniently and economically by suitable metallurgical processes.

3.1.4 Difference Between Mineral and Ore

BasisMineralOre
MeaningNaturally occurring mineral material containing a substance of interest.A mineral/source from which metal extraction is technically and economically feasible.
Economic importanceMay or may not be economically useful.Has sufficient economic value for extraction under suitable conditions.
ExampleMany minerals may contain metallic compounds.Bauxite is an important ore/source for aluminium; haematite is an important ore of iron.

3.2.1 Metallurgy

Definition — Metallurgy: Metallurgy is the science and technology concerned with the extraction of metals from their ores, their purification and, where required, their processing into useful forms.

3.2.3 Gangue

Definition — Gangue: Gangue is the unwanted earthy or rocky material associated with an ore.

3.2.4 Matrix

Definition — Matrix: Matrix is the unwanted rocky or earthy material in which the valuable mineral is embedded or associated in an ore deposit.

In introductory metallurgy texts, gangue and matrix are often used in closely related senses. Follow the terminology used by your teacher/textbook for a particular examination question.

3.2.5 Flux

Definition — Flux: A flux is a substance added during metallurgical processing to react with unwanted impurities and form a more easily removable slag.

3.2.6 Slag

Definition — Slag: Slag is the relatively fusible material formed by reaction between flux and gangue or other impurities during smelting or related high-temperature metallurgical operations.
Flux + Gangue → Slag

3.3

3.3 Ancient Indian Metallurgy (IKS)

Definition — Ancient Indian Metallurgy: Ancient Indian metallurgy refers to the historical knowledge and practices developed in the Indian subcontinent for obtaining, processing and using metals and metallic materials.

This section is explicitly marked as IKS (Indian Knowledge System) in the uploaded syllabus. The purpose in this course is to recognize the historical development of metallurgical knowledge alongside modern engineering concepts.

Major Historical Themes

  • Early knowledge of extraction and working of metals.
  • Use of copper, bronze and iron in tools, weapons, utensils and other objects.
  • Development of iron-working and steel-making traditions.
  • Evidence of skilled forging, casting and metal processing.
  • Historical examples demonstrate that metallurgical practice developed through observation, experimentation and craftsmanship.

Delhi Iron Pillar — Historical Example

Study point: The Delhi Iron Pillar is a well-known historical example frequently discussed in Indian metallurgy because of its composition, manufacturing history and notable resistance to atmospheric corrosion under its historical conditions.

For an examination answer, focus on the historical significance and the idea that ancient metalworkers possessed substantial practical knowledge. Avoid claiming a single modern mechanism as the complete explanation for every aspect of its corrosion behaviour unless your prescribed source specifically requires it.

Exam answer: Write definition → mention ancient Indian metalworking traditions → give one or two historical examples → connect the topic to development of engineering/material knowledge.

3.4 Extraction of Aluminium, Iron and Copper

The syllabus specifically requires extraction of aluminium, iron and copper from important ores along with reactions, properties and uses.

A. Extraction of Aluminium

Definition — Aluminium Extraction: Aluminium is obtained industrially from aluminium-bearing ore, principally bauxite, by first producing purified alumina and then electrolytically reducing molten alumina to aluminium.

Ore

Main ore/source: Bauxite, a hydrated aluminium-oxide-rich material.

Step 1 — Purification of Bauxite

A common industrial route is the Bayer process. The bauxite is treated with concentrated sodium hydroxide under controlled conditions so that aluminium compounds enter solution while many impurities are separated.

Simplified Bayer-process chemistry:
Al(OH)₃ + NaOH → Na[Al(OH)₄]
Na[Al(OH)₄] → Al(OH)₃↓ + NaOH
2Al(OH)₃ → Al₂O₃ + 3H₂O

The exact Bayer chemistry depends on the hydrated aluminium minerals present; the equations above represent the core textbook idea.

Step 2 — Electrolytic Reduction

Alumina is dissolved in molten cryolite-based electrolyte to lower the effective operating temperature and improve conductivity. Electrolysis is then used to produce aluminium at the cathode.

Properties of Aluminium

  • Low density compared with many common structural metals.
  • Good electrical and thermal conductivity.
  • Can be formed and fabricated readily.
  • Develops a protective oxide film under suitable conditions.

Uses of Aluminium

  • Electrical conductors and cables.
  • Transport components and lightweight structures.
  • Packaging and containers.
  • Construction and engineering components.

B. Extraction of Iron

Definition — Iron Extraction: Iron extraction is the metallurgical production of iron from iron ores by reduction at high temperature, commonly in a blast furnace for pig iron/hot metal production.

Important Ores

Haematite (Fe₂O₃), magnetite (Fe₃O₄) and siderite (FeCO₃) are important iron ores.

Properties and Uses of Iron

Iron is a strong, useful engineering metal and is the base metal of many ferrous alloys. Commercial iron-containing materials are widely used in structures, machinery, tools and manufacturing.

C. Extraction of Copper

Definition — Copper Extraction: Copper extraction is the recovery and purification of copper from copper-bearing ores through concentration, thermal/chemical conversion and refining operations.

Important Ores

Copper pyrites/chalcopyrite (CuFeS₂) is an important copper sulphide ore. Cuprite (Cu₂O) and malachite are also copper-bearing minerals.

Concentration

Sulphide copper ores are commonly concentrated by froth flotation before smelting.

Roasting and Smelting

Representative reactions used to illustrate the conversion of sulphide minerals include:

2Cu₂S + 3O₂ → 2Cu₂O + 2SO₂
2Cu₂O + Cu₂S → 6Cu + SO₂

Industrial copper extraction is more complex than these simplified equations and includes matte formation and controlled converting/refining steps.

Properties and Uses of Copper

  • Excellent electrical conductivity.
  • Good thermal conductivity.
  • Can be drawn into wires.
  • Used in electrical wiring, cables, heat exchangers, plumbing and alloys such as brass and bronze.

3.5 Alloys — Definition, Purpose, Ferrous and Non-Ferrous Alloys

Definition — Alloy: An alloy is a metallic material formed by combining two or more elements, at least one of which is a metal, to obtain useful properties.

Purpose of Alloying

  • Increase strength or hardness.
  • Improve corrosion resistance.
  • Improve wear resistance.
  • Modify melting point or machinability.
  • Improve electrical, magnetic or other functional properties.

Ferrous Alloys

Definition — Ferrous Alloy: A ferrous alloy is an alloy in which iron is the principal metallic constituent.
Alloy/materialTypical principal constituentsImportant propertiesApplications
Carbon steelFe + CStrength and hardness depend strongly on carbon contentStructures, machine parts, tools and general fabrication
Cast ironFe + relatively high C with Si and other constituentsGood castability, compressive strength and dampingMachine beds, housings, pipes and cast components
Stainless steelFe + Cr, often Ni and other alloying elementsGood corrosion resistanceUtensils, chemical equipment, medical and engineering components

Non-Ferrous Alloys

Definition — Non-Ferrous Alloy: A non-ferrous alloy is an alloy in which iron is not the principal metallic constituent.
AlloyTypical compositionProperties / uses
BrassCu + ZnGood formability and corrosion resistance; fittings, instruments, electrical hardware
BronzeCu + SnGood wear/corrosion resistance; bearings, marine and mechanical components
DuraluminAl + Cu with smaller additions such as Mg/MnLightweight and relatively strong; transport and structural applications
CupronickelCu + NiGood corrosion resistance; marine applications

3.6

3.6 Polymers — Types and Polymerization

Definition — Polymer: A polymer is a high-molecular-mass substance made up of many repeating structural units derived from smaller molecules called monomers.
Definition — Monomer: A monomer is a relatively small molecule capable of joining with other molecules of the same or different type to form a polymer.

Homopolymer

Definition — Homopolymer: A homopolymer is a polymer formed from repeating units derived from one type of monomer.

Example: Polyethylene is formed from ethene units.

Copolymer

Definition — Copolymer: A copolymer is a polymer whose chain contains structural units derived from two or more different monomers.
HomopolymerCopolymer
Based on one monomer typeBased on two or more different monomer types
Example: polyethyleneExample: Buna-type copolymers are formed from more than one monomer

Natural and Synthetic Polymers

Natural Polymer: A polymer occurring naturally in biological or natural systems.

Examples: cellulose, starch, proteins, natural rubber.

Synthetic Polymer: A polymer produced by chemical processing from suitable monomers.

Examples: polyethylene, PVC, nylon and Bakelite.

Addition Polymerization

Definition — Addition Polymerization: Addition polymerization is polymerization in which unsaturated monomer molecules add together without the regular elimination of a small molecule as a by-product.
n CH₂=CH₂ → [–CH₂–CH₂–]ₙ

Ethene → polyethylene

Condensation Polymerization

Definition — Condensation Polymerization: Condensation polymerization is polymerization in which monomers containing suitable functional groups combine with elimination of small molecules such as water, HCl or methanol, depending on the system.

Thermoplastic

Definition — Thermoplastic: A thermoplastic polymer softens on heating and can generally be reshaped repeatedly without undergoing the permanent cross-linking characteristic of thermosetting materials.

Examples include polyethylene and PVC.

Thermosetting Plastic

Definition — Thermosetting Plastic: A thermosetting polymer forms a permanently cross-linked structure during curing and does not soften for reshaping in the same way as a thermoplastic.

Example: Bakelite.

PropertyThermoplasticThermosetting
HeatingSoftens and can often be reshapedAfter curing, does not remelt for normal reshaping

3.7.1 Polyethylene

n CH₂=CH₂ → [–CH₂–CH₂–]ₙ

Polyethylene is widely used because of its low density, chemical resistance and useful processing characteristics.

3.7.2 PVC

n CH₂=CHCl → [–CH₂–CHCl–]ₙ

PVC is useful for pipes, insulation and many rigid/flexible products depending on formulation and additives.

3.7.3 Orlon

n CH₂=CH–CN → [–CH₂–CH(CN)–]ₙ

Orlon is the trade name historically associated with polyacrylonitrile fibre. It is used in textile applications and as a precursor for some high-performance carbon-fibre routes.

3.7.4 Terylene / PET

Ethylene glycol + terephthalic acid undergo condensation polymerization to form polyester chains with elimination of a small molecule.

n HO–CH₂–CH₂–OH + n HOOC–C₆H₄–COOH → polyester + water

3.7.5 Nylon 66

Hexamethylenediamine and adipic acid undergo condensation polymerization.

n H₂N–(CH₂)₆–NH₂ + n HOOC–(CH₂)₄–COOH → Nylon 66 + water

3.7.6 Nylon 6

Caprolactam → Nylon 6

Nylon 6 is produced by polymerization of caprolactam under suitable industrial conditions.

3.7.7 Bakelite

Phenol + formaldehyde → phenol-formaldehyde resin → Bakelite

Bakelite is a thermosetting phenol-formaldehyde material and is useful where electrical insulation and dimensional stability are required.

Exam trick: For each polymer remember Polymer → Monomer → Polymerization type → One use.

3.8 Natural Rubber and Vulcanization

Definition — Natural Rubber: Natural rubber is a natural polymer obtained mainly from latex of rubber-producing plants; chemically it is primarily cis-1,4-polyisoprene.

3.8.1 Monomer

Monomer — Isoprene: Isoprene is 2-methyl-1,3-butadiene, CH₂=C(CH₃)–CH=CH₂, the monomer from which natural rubber is derived.
n (isoprene) → cis-1,4-polyisoprene

3.8.2 Limitations of Raw Natural Rubber

Unmodified natural rubber can become soft and sticky at elevated temperatures and hard/brittle at low temperatures. Its mechanical properties and resistance to heat, solvents and wear may be insufficient for many engineering applications.

3.8.3 Vulcanization

Definition — Vulcanization: Vulcanization is the controlled chemical treatment of rubber, commonly with sulfur and heat, that introduces cross-links between polymer chains and improves useful properties.
Rubber polymer chains

3.8.4 Advantages of Vulcanized Rubber

  • Improved tensile strength and elasticity balance.
  • Better resistance to wear and mechanical deformation.
  • Improved temperature performance over untreated rubber.
  • Reduced stickiness.
  • Better durability for engineering applications.

Applications

Vulcanized rubber is used in tyres, tubes, belts, hoses, seals, gaskets, footwear and many other products.

⚡ Quick Revision

  • Ore: economically extractable source of a metal.
  • Gangue: unwanted earthy/rocky matter.
  • Flux + gangue → slag.
  • Metallurgy: concentration → conversion → extraction/reduction → refining.
  • Aluminium: bauxite → alumina → electrolytic reduction.
  • Iron: iron ore → blast furnace reduction → hot metal/pig iron → further processing.
  • Copper: sulphide ore → concentration → roasting/smelting/converting → refining.
  • Ferrous: iron is principal metal.
  • Non-ferrous: iron is not principal metal.
  • Homopolymer: one monomer type.
  • Copolymer: two or more monomer types.
  • Addition: unsaturated monomers add without regular small-molecule elimination.
  • Condensation: polymer formation accompanied by elimination of a small molecule in the polymer-forming reaction.
  • Thermoplastic: can soften and be reshaped on heating.
  • Thermosetting: permanently cross-linked after curing.
  • Natural rubber: cis-1,4-polyisoprene.
  • Vulcanization: cross-linking of rubber, commonly with sulfur.
