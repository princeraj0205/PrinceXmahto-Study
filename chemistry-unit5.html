5.1 Electrolyte, Nonelectrolyte and Conduction

Electrolyte

Definition: An electrolyte is a substance that produces ions when dissolved in water or present in a molten state and therefore conducts electricity through the movement of ions.

Examples: HCl, NaCl, KOH and many ionic compounds in aqueous/molten conditions.

Nonelectrolyte

Definition: A nonelectrolyte is a substance that does not produce sufficient ions in solution to conduct electricity appreciably.

Examples: sugar and ethanol in ordinary aqueous solution.

ElectrolyteNonelectrolyte
Produces ions in suitable medium.Does not appreciably produce ions.
Conducts electricity through ionic movement.Does not conduct appreciably by ions.
Examples: NaCl, HCl, KOH.Examples: sugar, ethanol.

Strong and weak electrolytes

Strong electrolyte: An electrolyte that ionizes or dissociates to a large extent in the specified solution.
Weak electrolyte: An electrolyte that ionizes only partially in the specified solution.

Electrolytic conduction

Definition: Electrolytic conduction is the conduction of electric current through an electrolyte by the movement of positive and negative ions.
DC source + - | | Anode Cathode | | | Na+ → | Na+ moves toward cathode | ← Cl- | Cl- moves toward anode ┌──────────────┐ │ electrolyte │ └──────────────┘

Metallic conduction

Definition: Metallic conduction is the flow of electric current through a metal due mainly to the movement of mobile electrons.
Electrolytic conductionMetallic conduction
Charge carriers are ions.Charge carriers are electrons.
Occurs in electrolytes in suitable conditions.Occurs in metals.
Often accompanied by chemical changes at electrodes.No chemical decomposition of the metal is required for ordinary conduction.
Temperature increase generally increases ionic conductivity up to relevant limits.Metal resistance generally increases with temperature.

Factors affecting electrolytic conductance

  • Concentration: Changing concentration changes the number and interactions of ions.
  • Nature of electrolyte: Strong and weak electrolytes behave differently.
  • Temperature: Temperature affects ion mobility and solvent properties.
  • Nature of solvent: Solvent viscosity and ability to support ion formation influence conduction.
  • Nature of ions: Ionic size, charge and mobility affect conductivity.
Exam Focus: Learn the difference between electrolytic and metallic conduction. A comparison table can answer a 3–5 mark question efficiently.

5.2 Molar Conductivity, Equivalent Conductivity & Kohlrausch's Law

Conductance

Definition: Conductance is the reciprocal of resistance and represents the ease with which electric current passes through a conductor.
G = 1/R

Unit: siemens (S).

Specific conductance / conductivity

Definition: Conductivity (specific conductance) is the conductance of a material or solution of specified geometry, conventionally related to the conductance of a cube of unit dimensions.
κ = G × (l/A)

Here κ is conductivity, G is conductance, l is length and A is cross-sectional area.

Molar conductivity

Definition: Molar conductivity is the conductance of the volume of solution containing one mole of electrolyte when placed between suitable electrodes.
Λm = κ × 1000 / C

When κ is expressed in S cm⁻¹ and C in mol L⁻¹, Λm is commonly expressed in S cm² mol⁻¹.

Equivalent conductivity

Definition: Equivalent conductivity is the conductance of the volume of solution containing one gram-equivalent of an electrolyte when placed between suitable electrodes.
Λeq = κ × 1000 / N

Here N is normality in equivalents per litre when the corresponding conventional units are used.

Variation of molar conductivity with dilution

As a solution is diluted, ions generally experience less interionic interaction and their effective mobility increases. Therefore molar conductivity generally increases with dilution.

Λm │ / │ ____/ strong electrolyte │ ____/ │ ____/ │ ____/ weak electrolyte: stronger rise │____/ └──────────────────────→ dilution

For strong electrolytes, the increase is comparatively gradual. Weak electrolytes can show a more pronounced increase because dilution promotes greater ionization.

Kohlrausch's law

Definition: Kohlrausch's law of independent migration of ions states that at infinite dilution, each ion contributes independently to the total molar conductivity of an electrolyte.
Λm° = ν+λ+° + ν−λ−°

ν represents the stoichiometric number of each ion and λ° represents limiting ionic conductivity.

Example: For a 1:1 electrolyte AB, Λ° = λ°(A⁺) + λ°(B⁻). For a 1:2 electrolyte AB₂, Λ° = λ°(A²⁺) + 2λ°(B⁻), using the appropriate ionic species and units.
Application: Kohlrausch's law is useful for calculating limiting molar conductivity and for obtaining quantities that are difficult to measure directly under ordinary conditions.

5.3 Faraday's Laws of Electrolysis

Electrolysis

Definition: Electrolysis is the process in which electrical energy drives a chemical reaction, usually by passing current through an electrolyte.
DC source + - | | ANODE CATHODE | | oxidation reduction | | ┌────────────────────┐ │ electrolyte │ └────────────────────┘

First law of electrolysis

Statement: The mass of a substance deposited or liberated at an electrode is directly proportional to the quantity of electricity passed through the electrolyte, provided other conditions are fixed.
m ∝ Q

Since Q = It:

m = ZIt

where m = mass deposited, I = current, t = time and Z = electrochemical equivalent.

Second law of electrolysis

Statement: When the same quantity of electricity passes through different electrolytes, the masses of substances deposited are proportional to their equivalent weights.
m₁/m₂ = E₁/E₂

Combined useful relation

m = (E × I × t) / F

where E is equivalent weight and F is the Faraday constant, approximately 96500 C mol⁻¹ in the common engineering-chemistry calculation convention.

Solved example: A current of 2 A is passed for 30 minutes. Charge passed:
Q = It = 2 × (30×60) = 3600 C.
The deposited mass can then be found from m = ZQ or m = EIt/F when E is given.
Exam Focus: Be ready to state both laws, derive m = ZIt, define electrochemical equivalent and solve mass-deposition numericals.

5.4 Galvanic Cell, Electrode Potential, SHE, EMF, Electrochemical Series & Nernst Equation

Galvanic / electrochemical cell

Definition: A galvanic cell is an electrochemical cell that converts the chemical energy of a spontaneous redox reaction into electrical energy.
e− flow through wire → ┌───────────────V───────────────┐ │ │ Zn electrode Cu electrode ANODE (-) CATHODE (+) │ │ Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻ Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu │ │ ┌───────┐ salt bridge ┌───────┐ │ ZnSO₄ │═════════════════════════│ CuSO₄ │ └───────┘ └───────┘ DANIELL / Zn-Cu cell idea

Anode and cathode

Anode: The electrode at which oxidation occurs.
Cathode: The electrode at which reduction occurs.

Memory rule: AN OX = Anode Oxidation; RED CAT = Reduction Cathode. The signs of electrodes depend on whether the cell is galvanic or electrolytic.

Electrode potential

Definition: Electrode potential is the potential difference established between an electrode and its electrolyte due to the tendency of the electrode species to undergo oxidation or reduction.

Standard Hydrogen Electrode (SHE)

Definition: The standard hydrogen electrode is a reference electrode assigned a standard electrode potential of 0.00 V under standard conditions.
H₂ gas ↓ Pt electrode │ H⁺ solution │ reference electrode Ideal standard conditions: H₂ pressure ≈ 1 bar H⁺ activity ≈ 1 temperature specified by convention

The SHE is used as a reference to measure standard electrode potentials of other half-cells.

Cell EMF

Definition: The electromotive force (EMF) of a cell is the potential difference between the two electrodes when the cell is not delivering current, under the specified conditions.
E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode

Electrochemical series

Definition: The electrochemical series is an arrangement of elements or ions according to their standard reduction electrode potentials.

It helps compare tendencies for reduction/oxidation and is useful in assessing cell reactions and relative oxidizing/reducing behavior.

Nernst equation

Definition: The Nernst equation relates electrode or cell potential to temperature and the activities/concentrations/pressures of the reacting species under non-standard conditions.
E = E° − (RT/nF) ln Q

At 25 °C, using common logarithms:

E = E° − (0.0591/n) log Q

where E = electrode/cell potential, E° = standard potential, R = gas constant, T = absolute temperature, n = number of electrons transferred, F = Faraday constant and Q = reaction quotient.

Step-by-step Nernst example

Suppose: E° = 0.50 V, n = 2 and Q = 100 at 25 °C.
E = 0.50 − (0.0591/2)log(100)
log(100) = 2
E = 0.50 − 0.0591 = 0.4409 V.
The important exam skill is identifying n and Q correctly before substitution.
Cell feasibility: Under the usual thermodynamic convention, a spontaneous cell reaction under specified conditions has a positive cell potential and corresponding negative Gibbs energy change. Use the actual conditions given in the question rather than assuming standard conditions.

5.5 Batteries and Fuel Cells

Battery

Definition: A battery is an electrochemical source that converts stored chemical energy into electrical energy through one or more electrochemical cells.

5.5.1 Primary cell — Dry cell

Definition: A primary cell is an electrochemical cell intended for use until its reactants are substantially consumed and is not normally designed for convenient electrical recharging.

The conventional zinc-carbon dry cell uses a zinc container as anode, a central carbon rod associated with the cathode mixture, and an electrolyte paste containing ammonium chloride/zinc chloride in traditional formulations.

Carbon rod │ ┌─────┴─────┐ │ cathode │ │ mixture │ │ electrolyte│ │ paste │ └───────────┘ Zinc container anode

5.5.2 Secondary cell — Lead storage battery

Definition: A secondary cell is an electrochemical cell designed so that its chemical reaction can be substantially reversed by passing current in the opposite direction, allowing recharge.

A lead-acid battery uses lead dioxide and lead electrodes in sulfuric acid electrolyte. During discharge, the electrodes undergo chemical changes that can be reversed substantially during charging.

FeatureLead-acid battery
Negative electrode during dischargePb
Positive electrode during dischargePbO₂
ElectrolyteH₂SO₄ solution
Rechargeable?Yes
ApplicationsAutomotive starting, backup and other high-current applications

5.5.3 Fuel cell

Definition: A fuel cell is an electrochemical device that converts the chemical energy of a continuously supplied fuel and oxidant directly into electrical energy.
Fuel → [ ANODE | ELECTROLYTE | CATHODE ] ← Oxidant ↓ electrons → external circuit ↓ Electrical power + products

Hydrogen-oxygen fuel cells are an important example. In an ideal overall reaction, hydrogen and oxygen form water while electrical energy is produced.

2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O

5.6 Corrosion — Dry, Wet and Prevention

Corrosion

Definition: Corrosion is the gradual deterioration of a metal or material due to chemical or electrochemical interaction with its environment.

5.6.1 Dry corrosion

Definition: Dry corrosion is corrosion caused by direct chemical reaction of a metal with dry gases, usually without an aqueous electrolyte.

Examples include oxidation of metals at elevated temperature and chemical attack by reactive gases.

5.6.2 Wet corrosion

Definition: Wet corrosion is electrochemical corrosion occurring in the presence of moisture or an aqueous electrolyte, involving anodic and cathodic reactions.
Metal surface + moisture ANODIC AREA CATHODIC AREA M → Mⁿ⁺ + ne⁻ O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ → 4OH⁻ │ ↑ └──── electrons through metal ─────┘ Electrolyte/moisture provides ionic path.

Rusting of iron — simplified electrochemical picture

At anodic sites:

Fe → Fe²⁺ + 2e⁻

At cathodic sites in aerated neutral water, a representative reduction is:

O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ → 4OH⁻

Then Fe²⁺ and hydroxide species undergo further reactions leading ultimately to hydrated iron(III) oxide, commonly called rust.

Conditions promoting corrosion

  • Presence of moisture
  • Oxygen or other oxidizing agents
  • Dissolved salts/electrolytes
  • Acidic or otherwise aggressive environments
  • Contact between dissimilar metals under suitable electrolyte conditions
  • Surface imperfections and differential aeration

Methods of prevention

MethodBasic ideaExample
Painting/coatingIsolate metal from moisture and oxygen.Painted steel
Oiling/greasingCreate a protective barrier.Machine parts
GalvanizingCoat iron/steel with zinc.Zinc-coated sheets
ElectroplatingDeposit a protective metal layer electrolytically.Chromium/nickel plating
AlloyingModify composition to improve corrosion resistance.Stainless steels
Cathodic protectionMake the protected metal act as a cathode.Sacrificial anode systems
InhibitorsReduce corrosion rate by controlled addition of chemicals.Closed systems
Exam Focus: Clearly distinguish dry and wet corrosion. For wet corrosion, show anodic oxidation, cathodic reduction and electron/ion movement. Then write at least four prevention methods.
