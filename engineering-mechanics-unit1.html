1.1 Significance and Relevance

Mechanics

Mechanics is the branch of engineering science dealing with bodies at rest or in motion under the action of forces.

Applied Mechanics

Applied mechanics applies the principles of mechanics to practical engineering problems such as beams, machines and force systems.

Statics and Dynamics

StaticsDynamics
Deals with bodies at rest/equilibrium.Deals with bodies in motion.
Example: loaded beam at rest.Example: moving vehicle.

1.2 Basic Terms

Space

The geometric region in which positions of bodies are described.

Time

Measure of duration and sequence of events. SI unit: second (s).

Mass

Quantity of matter in a body. SI unit: kilogram (kg).

Particle

An idealized body whose dimensions are negligible compared with the distances involved.

Body

A physical object having mass and occupying space.

Rigid Body

An idealized body in which the distance between any two points remains constant when forces act.

1.3 Scalar, Vector and Units

Scalar Quantity

A quantity having magnitude only. Examples: mass, time, temperature, length and speed.

Vector Quantity

A quantity having both magnitude and direction. Examples: force, displacement, velocity and acceleration.

1.4 Force

Definition and Unit

Force is an external agency that tends to change the state of rest or motion of a body, or change its shape. Force is a vector.

F = ma     1 N = 1 kg·m/s²

Representation and Characteristics

A force is represented by an arrow. Its important characteristics are magnitude, direction, line of action and point of application.

             F →
       A ●────────────● B
       point       line of action

Bow's Notation

Bow's notation identifies forces in graphical force diagrams by labelling the spaces/regions around the structure and naming a force by the two adjacent letters.

Effects of Force

  • Starts or stops motion.
  • Changes velocity or direction.
  • Produces rotation.
  • Changes shape or size.

Principle of Transmissibility

For a rigid body, a force may be moved anywhere along its own line of action without changing its external effect on the body.

Original: A ●────────────► F

1.5 Resolution of a Force and Moment

Resolution

Resolution means replacing one force by components whose combined effect is equivalent to the original force.

Orthogonal Components

             F
            /|
           / |
          /  | Fy
         /θ  |
        /____|
          Fx
Fx = F cosθ       Fy = F sinθ
F = √(Fx²+Fy²)   tanθ = Fy/Fx
Example: Resolve 100 N at 30° to horizontal.
Fx = 100 cos30° = 86.6 N
Fy = 100 sin30° = 50 N.

Non-Orthogonal Components

Components that are not perpendicular are non-orthogonal. Their geometry must be considered; do not automatically use the rectangular-component formulas.

Moment of a Force

Moment is the turning effect of a force about a point.

M = F × d

d is the perpendicular distance from the reference point to the line of action. Unit: N·m.

                F ↓
                │
O ●─────────────┼
      distance d
Moment about O = Fd

Moment may be clockwise or anticlockwise.

Avignon's Theorem

For a force resolved into components, the moment of the original force about a point equals the algebraic sum of the moments of its components about that point.

M(F) = M(Fx) + M(Fy)

1.6 Composition of Forces

Resultant

The resultant is a single force having the same external effect as a given force system.

Two Concurrent Forces

R = √(P² + Q² + 2PQ cosθ)
tanα = Q sinθ / (P + Q cosθ)

Law of Parallelogram of Forces

If two concurrent forces are represented by two adjacent sides of a parallelogram, the diagonal through their common point represents their resultant.

          B
         /|
        / |
       /  | Q
      /   |
     O────A
       P
Diagonal OB = resultant R

Law of Triangle of Forces

If two forces are represented by two sides of a triangle taken in order, the third side taken in the opposite direction represents their resultant.

Law of Polygon of Forces

If several forces are represented by the sides of a polygon taken in order, the closing side represents the resultant.

Component Method

ΣFx = algebraic sum of horizontal components
ΣFy = algebraic sum of vertical components
R = √[(ΣFx)² + (ΣFy)²]
tanθ = ΣFy / ΣFx
Example 1: 30 N and 40 N perpendicular.
R = √(30²+40²) = 50 N.
Example 2: 20 N and 30 N at 60°.
R = √(20²+30²+2×20×30×0.5) = √1900 ≈ 43.6 N.

1.7 Graphic Statics

Meaning

Graphic statics uses scaled graphical constructions to analyse forces and determine resultants/equilibrium conditions.

Graphical Representation

Choose a force scale, draw a line whose length represents magnitude, and use the arrow for direction.

Scale: 1 cm = 10 N
50 N → 5 cm line

Space Diagram

Shows the actual arrangement of the body, loads, supports and lines of action.

Force Diagram

Shows the forces separately with their directions and relative magnitudes.

Polar Diagram

A graphical construction using a selected pole and rays associated with a force polygon, used with the funicular construction.

Funicular Polygon

A graphical construction used to determine the resultant, especially for parallel force systems, including its line of action.

Graphical Resultant — Concurrent Forces

  1. Select a scale.
  2. Draw the first force to scale.
  3. Draw remaining forces head-to-tail.
  4. Join the starting point to the final point.
  5. The closing line gives resultant magnitude and direction.
O ─────► A
         \
          \────► B
                 \
                  \────► C
O to C = resultant

Graphical Resultant — Parallel Forces

Use the force polygon together with the polar construction and funicular polygon to locate the resultant and its line of action.

Common Mistakes

  • Force is a vector, not a scalar.
  • Mass is measured in kg; force in N.
  • Moment uses perpendicular distance.
  • Transmissibility permits movement along the same line of action, not to an arbitrary parallel line.
  • Draw a force diagram before resolving forces.
  • For graphical solutions always state the scale.
