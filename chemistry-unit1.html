1.1 Atoms and Its Fundamental Particles

📖 Book Definitions — 1.1

Matter: Anything that has mass and occupies space.

Atom: The basic unit of an element that retains the chemical identity of that element.

Electron: A negatively charged subatomic particle associated with the region outside the atomic nucleus.

Proton: A positively charged subatomic particle present in the nucleus.

Neutron: An electrically neutral subatomic particle present in the nucleus.

Nucleus: The very small, dense central region of an atom containing protons and neutrons.

Atomic Number (Z): The number of protons present in the nucleus of an atom.

Mass Number (A): The total number of protons and neutrons present in the nucleus.

Isotopes: Atoms of the same element having the same atomic number but different mass numbers.

Definition: An atom is the basic unit used to describe the structure and chemical identity of an element. It contains a central nucleus and electrons associated with the region outside it.

Conceptual atomic structure

Nucleus contains protons and neutrons; electrons occupy the region outside the nucleus.

Atomic number

Atomic number is the number of protons in the nucleus.

Z = P

Mass number

Mass number is the total number of protons and neutrons.

A = P + N    and    N = A − Z

Worked example

An atom has Z = 17 and A = 35.

Protons = 17. For a neutral atom, electrons = 17.

Neutrons = A − Z = 35 − 17 = 18.

Isotopes

Atoms of the same element have the same atomic number but may have different neutron numbers and hence different mass numbers.

Exam focus: three particles, atomic number, mass number, neutron calculation and basic atomic structure.

1.2 Rutherford Model of Atom

📖 Book Definitions — 1.2

Rutherford Atomic Model: A nuclear model of the atom in which a very small, dense, positively charged nucleus is surrounded by the region occupied by electrons.

Alpha Particle: A positively charged particle used in Rutherford's scattering experiment.

Alpha-Particle Scattering: The deflection of alpha particles when they pass through a thin metal foil.

Nuclear Model: A model in which the atom has a small central nucleus containing concentrated positive charge and most of its mass.

Purpose

Rutherford investigated atomic structure through alpha-particle scattering using a very thin metal foil and a fluorescent observation screen.

Alpha source  ───────►  | THIN FOIL |  ─────────►  Screen
                         ↗   ↑   ↘
                    scattered alpha particles

Conceptual experimental arrangement

Observations

1

Most alpha particles passed straight through.

2

Some were deflected through small angles.

3

A very small number were deflected through large angles.

4

A very small fraction returned backwards.

Conclusions

1

Most of an atom is empty space.

2

Positive charge is concentrated in a small region.

3

The small, dense central region is the nucleus.

4

Most atomic mass is concentrated in the nucleus.

Limitations

  • Did not adequately explain atomic stability.
  • Did not explain the line spectra of atoms.
  • Did not provide the quantized energy-level description later introduced by Bohr.
5-mark answer order: experiment → observations → conclusions → nuclear model → limitations.

1.3 Bohr's Theory and Hydrogen Spectrum

📖 Book Definitions — 1.3

Bohr's Atomic Model: A model in which electrons occupy permitted stationary states having definite energies.

Stationary State: A permitted atomic state in which an electron has a definite energy and does not continuously radiate energy.

Energy Level: A permitted energy state of an electron in an atom.

Photon: A quantum of electromagnetic radiation associated with a definite amount of energy.

Hydrogen Spectrum: The characteristic set of spectral lines produced by hydrogen due to transitions between allowed energy states.

Spectral Series: A group of related spectral lines corresponding to transitions ending at the same lower energy level.

Why Bohr?

Rutherford established the nucleus but did not explain atomic stability and line spectra. Bohr introduced permitted stationary energy states.

Postulates

1

Electrons can occupy certain permitted stationary states.

2

Each permitted state has a definite energy.

3

An electron in an allowed stationary state does not continuously radiate energy.

4

Energy is absorbed or emitted when an electron changes between permitted states.

ΔE = hν
mvr = nh / 2π
Eₙ = −13.6/n² eV

Hydrogen spectrum

A hydrogen spectral line is associated with a transition between allowed energy levels. The photon energy equals the energy difference between the two states.

1.4 Wave Mechanical Model, de Broglie and Heisenberg

📖 Book Definitions — 1.4

Wave Mechanical Model: A modern description of atomic electrons based on wave behaviour and quantum-mechanical probability rather than fixed classical paths.

Matter Wave: The wave associated with a moving material particle.

de Broglie Wavelength: The wavelength associated with a moving particle, given by λ = h/p.

Momentum: The product of mass and velocity of a particle, p = mv.

Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle: The principle stating that the position and momentum of a microscopic particle cannot both be known with unlimited precision simultaneously.

Uncertainty: The range or lack of exactness associated with a measured physical quantity.

de Broglie relationship

de Broglie proposed that a moving material particle has an associated wavelength.

λ = h/p = h/mv

Here λ is wavelength, h is Planck constant, p is momentum, m is mass and v is velocity.

Meaning: wavelength is inversely proportional to momentum. Greater momentum → smaller matter wavelength.

Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle

Statement: Position and momentum of a microscopic particle cannot both be known with unlimited precision simultaneously.
Δx Δp ≥ h/4π

Numerical method

1. Write the given Δx or Δp. 2. Write ΔxΔp ≥ h/4π. 3. Rearrange for the unknown. 4. Substitute SI units. 5. Give the final unit.

One-line memory: de Broglie = wave nature of matter; Heisenberg = position-momentum uncertainty.

1.5 Quantum Numbers and Shapes of Atomic Orbitals

📖 Book Definitions — 1.5

Quantum Number: A number used to describe a particular property or state of an electron in an atom.

Principal Quantum Number (n): The quantum number that identifies the principal shell or energy level.

Azimuthal Quantum Number (l): The quantum number that identifies the subshell and is related to orbital shape.

Magnetic Quantum Number (mₗ): The quantum number that describes the orientation of an orbital.

Spin Quantum Number (mₛ): The quantum number that specifies the spin state of an electron.

Shell: A principal energy level identified by n.

Subshell: A subdivision of a shell identified by l, such as s, p or d.

Orbital: A region of space associated with a particular allowed electron state.

Quantum numbers specify the state of an electron and describe shell, subshell, orbital orientation and spin.

1.6 Pauli, Hund, Aufbau and Electronic Configuration

📖 Book Definitions — 1.6

Electronic Configuration: The arrangement of electrons among the shells, subshells and orbitals of an atom.

Aufbau Principle: The principle that electrons are assigned to available orbitals in increasing order of orbital energy.

Pauli's Exclusion Principle: An orbital can contain at most two electrons and their spin states must be opposite.

Hund's Rule of Maximum Multiplicity: Electrons occupy orbitals of equal energy singly before pairing, in the ground-state filling process.

Ground State: The lowest-energy electronic state of an atom.

Aufbau Principle

Electrons are assigned to orbitals in increasing order of energy, subject to the applicable rules.

1s → 2s → 2p → 3s → 3p → 4s → 3d

Pauli's Exclusion Principle

An orbital holds a maximum of two electrons and the two have opposite spin states.

Hund's Rule

Orbitals of equal energy are occupied singly before pairing in the ground-state filling process.

1.7 Concept of Chemical Bonding

📖 Book Definitions — 1.7

Chemical Bond: An interaction that holds atoms or ions together in a chemical species.

Ionic Bond: Electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions produced by electron transfer.

Covalent Bond: A bond formed through sharing of electron pairs between atoms.

Polar Covalent Bond: A covalent bond in which the shared electrons are distributed unequally between unlike atoms.

Non-polar Covalent Bond: A covalent bond in which the shared electrons are not preferentially pulled toward one atom in the simple bonding model.

Coordinate Bond: A covalent bond in which the shared electron pair is supplied by one atom.

Electrostatic Attraction: The attractive force between oppositely charged particles.

Why bonding?

Chemical bonding describes interactions that hold atoms or ions together in compounds. The syllabus requires ionic, covalent, polar/non-polar covalent and coordinate bonding.

Ionic bond

Ionic bonding is the electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions produced by electron transfer.

NaCl

1

Na loses one electron → Na⁺.

2

Cl gains one electron → Cl⁻.

3

Na⁺ and Cl⁻ attract → NaCl.

Na → Na⁺ + e⁻    Cl + e⁻ → Cl⁻

CaCl₂ and MgO

CaCl₂ represents charge balance between Ca²⁺ and two Cl⁻ ions. MgO represents Mg²⁺ and O²⁻ in the ionic model.

Covalent bond

A covalent bond involves sharing of electron pairs between atoms.
TypeExamples in syllabusBasic idea
Non-polar covalentH₂, F₂Identical atoms share without a bond polarity in the simple model.
Polar covalentHF, HClDifferent atoms attract shared electrons unequally.

Coordinate bond

A coordinate bond is a shared electron-pair bond in which the pair is supplied by one atom.

The syllabus lists CO, NH₄⁺, O₃ and H₂SO₄ for this topic. In an exam, draw the structure using the convention taught by your faculty and mark the donor contribution where required.

One-line comparison: ionic = transfer/ions; covalent = sharing; coordinate = shared pair donated by one atom.

1.8 Dipole Moment and Hydrogen Bonding

📖 Book Definitions — 1.8

Electronegativity: The tendency of an atom in a chemical bond to attract shared electrons toward itself.

Dipole: A system having separated positive and negative charge centres.

Dipole Moment: A measure of charge separation in a dipole, represented in the simple model by μ = qr.

Hydrogen Bond: A relatively strong attractive interaction involving hydrogen attached to a strongly electronegative atom and an electron-rich site.

Dipole moment

Unequal distribution of shared electrons can produce charge separation. Dipole moment measures the charge separation of a dipole.

μ = q × r

q = magnitude of charge; r = separation distance.

NH₃ and NF₃

The syllabus specifically includes NH₃ and NF₃. Molecular dipole depends on the individual bond dipoles and their vector combination according to molecular geometry.

Hydrogen bonding

Hydrogen bonding is a relatively strong attraction involving hydrogen attached to a strongly electronegative atom and an electron-rich site.

It is important in explaining characteristic properties of substances containing suitable hydrogen bonds. The syllabus identifies hydrogen bonding separately under Unit 1.

1.9 Solution and Strength of Solution

📖 Book Definitions — 1.9

Solution: A homogeneous mixture in which one or more solutes are uniformly distributed in a solvent.

Solute: The substance that is dissolved in a solution.

Solvent: The component that dissolves the solute and forms the medium of the solution.

Molarity: The number of moles of solute present per litre of solution.

Normality: The number of gram-equivalents of solute present per litre of solution.

Molality: The number of moles of solute present per kilogram of solvent.

Concentration: A quantitative measure of the amount of solute present in a specified amount of solution or solvent, depending on the concentration unit.

Solution, solute and solvent

A solution is a homogeneous mixture. The solute is dissolved; the solvent is the medium that dissolves it.
TermExample in salt-water solution
SoluteNaCl
SolventWater
SolutionSalt solution

Molarity

M = moles of solute / volume of solution (L)
Example: 0.5 mol in 2 L → M = 0.5/2 = 0.25 M.

Normality

N = gram-equivalents / volume of solution (L)
Example: 0.20 gram-equivalent in 0.50 L → N = 0.20/0.50 = 0.40 N.

Molality

m = moles of solute / mass of solvent (kg)
Example: 0.30 mol in 1.5 kg solvent → m = 0.30/1.5 = 0.20 mol kg⁻¹.

Preparing a given concentration — general sequence

Identify required concentration and final volume.

2

3

4

5

6

Most common mistake: M and N use volume of solution; molality uses mass of solvent.

📖 Book Definitions — 1.10

Indian Knowledge System (IKS): The curriculum component through which selected Indian knowledge traditions and historical ideas are studied.

Acharya Kanad: An ancient Indian thinker associated with the Vaisheshika philosophical tradition and its discussion of the nature of matter.

Anu: A concept of an extremely small particle in the historical philosophical discussion associated with the Vaisheshika tradition.

The SBTE Unit-1 syllabus includes Acharya Kanad under Indian Knowledge System (IKS).

Historical idea

Acharya Kanad is associated with the Vaisheshika philosophical tradition and the concept of extremely small particles, commonly discussed as anu.

Important: Study this as a historical/philosophical component of the IKS syllabus. It should be distinguished from experimentally developed modern atomic theory.
