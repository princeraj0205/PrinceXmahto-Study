4.1 General Characteristics of Solid State

4.1.1 What is a solid?

Definition: A solid is a form of matter in which constituent particles are held together by comparatively strong attractive forces and have limited freedom of movement.

In a solid, atoms, ions or molecules are not completely motionless. They vibrate about their mean positions. Because the attractive forces are strong, the particles generally cannot move freely from one place to another.

Crystalline and amorphous solids

Crystalline solid: A solid in which constituent particles are arranged in a regular, repeating three-dimensional pattern over long distances.

4.2 Classification of Crystalline Solids

Crystalline solids are classified according to the nature of particles and the forces holding those particles together.

Molecular solids

Definition: Crystalline solids whose lattice points are occupied by molecules.

Intermolecular forces may be dispersion forces, dipole-dipole forces or hydrogen bonding.

Examples: iodine crystals, solid CO₂, ice (hydrogen-bonded molecular solid).

Typical behavior: many have relatively low melting points and poor electrical conductivity.

Ionic solids

Definition: Crystalline solids containing positive and negative ions arranged in a lattice and held by electrostatic attraction.

Examples: NaCl, KCl, MgO.

They are generally hard and brittle. They conduct electricity when molten or dissolved in water because ions become mobile.

Metallic solids

Definition: Crystalline solids in which metal atoms/positive cores are held together by metallic bonding involving delocalized electrons.

Examples: Cu, Al, Fe.

They generally show electrical and thermal conductivity, malleability and ductility.

Covalent solids

Definition: Crystalline solids in which atoms are joined by an extended network of strong covalent bonds.

Examples: diamond, silicon, silicon carbide.

They are generally hard and have high melting points. Graphite is an important exception in electrical conductivity because of delocalized electrons between layers.

4.3 Crystal Lattice and Unit Cells — Primitive, BCC and FCC

Crystal lattice

Definition: A crystal lattice is a regular three-dimensional arrangement of points representing equivalent positions of particles in a crystalline solid.

Unit cell

Definition: A unit cell is the smallest repeating structural unit of a crystal lattice which, when repeated in three dimensions, generates the crystal structure.
Crystal lattice idea ●───●───●───● │ │ │ │ ●───●───●───● │ │ │ │ ●───●───●───● One repeating block = unit cell

Important cubic unit cells

Unit cellParticle positionsNumber of effective particles per cellCoordination number
Primitive / simple cubic (SC)Corners only16
Body-centred cubic (BCC)Corners + one body-centre particle28
Face-centred cubic (FCC)Corners + one particle at each face centre412

How do we count particles?

A corner particle is shared by 8 unit cells, so its contribution is 1/8. A face-centred particle is shared by 2 cells, so its contribution is 1/2. A body-centred particle belongs completely to one cell, so its contribution is 1.

Example — BCC: 8 corners × 1/8 = 1 effective particle. One body-centre particle × 1 = 1. Total = 2 particles per unit cell.
Example — FCC: 8 corners × 1/8 = 1 and 6 faces × 1/2 = 3. Total = 4 particles per unit cell.

Radius-edge relationships for cubic cells

Simple cubic: a = 2r
BCC: √3 a = 4r → a = 4r/√3
FCC: √2 a = 4r → a = 2√2 r

Here a is edge length and r is atomic radius. These relationships are useful in numerical problems.

Density of a unit cell

Density ρ = (Z × M) / (NA × a³)
  • Z = number of effective particles per unit cell
  • M = molar mass
  • NA = Avogadro constant
  • a³ = unit-cell volume for a cubic cell
Numerical idea: If a metal has an FCC unit cell, use Z = 4. Convert a to cm if density is required in g cm⁻³. Substitute all quantities with consistent units.
Common mistake: Do not write Z = 8 for a cubic cell just because there are 8 corners. Corner particles are shared, so their effective contribution is only 1 in total.

4.4 Imperfections of Solids — Point Defects

Definition — Crystal defect: A crystal defect is an irregularity or deviation from the ideal periodic arrangement of particles in a crystalline solid.

Real crystals are not perfectly ideal. Defects can affect density, colour, electrical properties, ionic conductivity and mechanical behavior.

Point defects

Definition: A point defect is a localized imperfection associated with one lattice point or a small region around it.

4.4.1 Stoichiometric defects

Stoichiometric defects do not change the overall stoichiometric ratio of the compound.

Vacancy defect

Definition: A vacancy defect occurs when an atom or ion is missing from its normal lattice site.

In an ionic solid, if equal numbers of positive and negative ions are missing, electrical neutrality can be maintained.

Interstitial defect

Definition: An interstitial defect occurs when an extra particle occupies a normally vacant interstitial site.

Frenkel defect

Definition: Frenkel defect is a dislocation defect in which a smaller ion, usually a cation, leaves its normal site and occupies an interstitial position.
Normal: Frenkel defect: + - + + - + - + - - - + - + + - + ↑ cation in interstitial site

Because an ion moves rather than leaving the crystal completely, density generally remains essentially unchanged.

Schottky defect

Definition: Schottky defect is a vacancy defect in which equal numbers of cations and anions are absent from their normal lattice sites.

Electrical neutrality is maintained, while the mass per unit volume decreases, so density decreases.

SchottkyFrenkel
Ions are missing from normal sites.An ion moves to an interstitial site.
Density decreases.Density is generally unchanged.
Common in crystals where ions have comparable sizes.Common when a smaller ion can fit into an interstitial site.

4.4.2 Impurity defects

Definition: An impurity defect is produced when foreign atoms or ions are introduced into a crystal lattice.

Impurities may occupy normal lattice sites or interstitial sites. In ionic crystals, aliovalent impurity ions can create vacancies to maintain electrical neutrality.

4.4.3 Non-stoichiometric defects

Definition: Non-stoichiometric defects are defects in which the ratio of constituent elements deviates from the ideal chemical formula.

Two important categories often discussed are metal excess and metal deficiency defects.

Metal excess due to anion vacancies

An anion vacancy can trap an electron. Such an electron-filled vacancy is commonly called an F-centre. These defects can impart colour to crystals.

F-centre: An anion vacancy occupied by an electron in an ionic crystal.

Metal deficiency

Metal deficiency can arise when some metal ions are missing and charge balance is maintained by ions of the same element in a higher oxidation state.

Exam Focus: Learn definitions and the difference between Schottky and Frenkel defects. Also know the three syllabus categories: stoichiometric, impurity and non-stoichiometric defects.

4.5 Electrical Properties — Metals and Semiconductors: Band Theory

Electrical conductivity

Definition: Electrical conductivity is the ability of a material to allow electric charge to flow through it.

Band theory — basic idea

In an isolated atom, electrons occupy discrete energy levels. When a very large number of atoms come together to form a solid, their energy levels split into a very large number of closely spaced levels. These groups of levels form energy bands.

Energy ↑ Conduction band ══════════════════ Band gap ─────────────── Valence band ══════════════════ For a conductor, bands may overlap or the highest occupied band may be partially filled.
Valence band: The energy band containing the valence electrons in a solid.
Conduction band: An energy band in which electrons can move through the solid and contribute to electrical conduction.
Forbidden energy gap: The energy interval between the valence band and conduction band in which electron energy states are not allowed.

Conductors, semiconductors and insulators

MaterialBand pictureConductivityExamples
ConductorValence and conduction bands overlap or conduction band is partially filledHighCu, Al
SemiconductorSmall forbidden gapIntermediate; strongly temperature dependentSi, Ge
InsulatorLarge forbidden gapVery low under ordinary conditionsGlass, rubber

Semiconductors

Definition: A semiconductor is a material whose electrical conductivity lies between that of a good conductor and an insulator and whose conductivity can be strongly influenced by temperature and impurities.

Intrinsic semiconductor

A pure semiconductor is called an intrinsic semiconductor. In it, electrons and holes are generated thermally, and the number of electrons is approximately equal to the number of holes.

Extrinsic semiconductor

A semiconductor whose conductivity is modified by adding a small controlled amount of impurity is called an extrinsic semiconductor.

TypeDoping ideaMajority carrier
n-typeAdd donor impurity to provide extra electronsElectrons
p-typeAdd acceptor impurity to create holesHoles
Electronics connection: Silicon is widely used in semiconductor devices. Controlled impurity addition allows its conductivity to be tailored for electronic components.

4.6 Magnetic Properties

Definition — Magnetism: Magnetism is the set of physical phenomena associated with magnetic moments and the response of matter to a magnetic field.

The syllabus asks for five categories: diamagnetism, paramagnetism, ferromagnetism, antiferromagnetism and ferrimagnetism.

4.6.1 Diamagnetism

Definition: A diamagnetic substance has no permanent net magnetic moment from unpaired electrons and is weakly repelled by an external magnetic field.

All electrons are paired in the idealized simple picture. The induced magnetic moment is opposite to the applied field.

4.6.2 Paramagnetism

Definition: A paramagnetic substance contains unpaired electrons and is weakly attracted by an external magnetic field.

Without an external field, magnetic moments are largely randomly oriented. The attraction is weak.

4.6.3 Ferromagnetism

Definition: Ferromagnetism is a strong form of magnetism in which magnetic moments can align cooperatively in the same direction, producing strong magnetization.

Examples include iron, cobalt and nickel under suitable conditions. Ferromagnetic materials can retain magnetization after the external field is removed.

4.6.4 Antiferromagnetism

Definition: Antiferromagnetism is magnetic ordering in which neighboring magnetic moments align oppositely, giving a small or zero net magnetic moment in the ideal case.
Antiferromagnetic arrangement ↑ ↓ ↑ ↓ ↑ ↓

4.6.5 Ferrimagnetism

Definition: Ferrimagnetism is magnetic ordering with oppositely directed magnetic moments of unequal magnitudes, producing a net magnetic moment.
Ferrimagnetic arrangement ↑ ↑ ↑ ↓ ↓ (net moment remains because magnitudes/number differ)
TypeResponse to fieldBasic magnetic arrangement

4.7 X-Ray Diffraction Method — Bragg's Law

X-rays and crystals

Definition — X-ray diffraction: X-ray diffraction is a technique in which X-rays interact with the regularly spaced atoms/planes of a crystalline material and produce a diffraction pattern that can be used to study crystal structure.

Crystal planes act as regularly spaced reflecting/diffracting layers. At certain angles, waves from successive planes reinforce each other. This is constructive interference.

Incident X-rays \ \ \ \ \ \ ─────────\───\──────── Plane 1 \ \ ───────────\───\────── Plane 2 ↑ path difference For constructive interference: path difference = nλ

Bragg's law

Bragg's law: The condition for constructive interference of X-rays reflected from successive parallel crystal planes is expressed as nλ = 2d sin θ.
nλ = 2d sin θ
  • n = order of diffraction (1, 2, 3, ...)
  • λ = wavelength of X-ray
  • d = spacing between crystal planes
  • θ = Bragg angle

Derivation idea

Consider two rays incident on two adjacent parallel planes. The lower ray travels an extra distance on entering and leaving the lower plane. Geometry gives the total path difference as 2d sin θ. For constructive interference, this path difference must be an integral multiple of wavelength:

2d sin θ = nλ

Step-by-step numerical

Question: X-rays of wavelength 0.154 nm give first-order diffraction at θ = 30°. Find d.

For first order, n = 1.
nλ = 2d sinθ
d = nλ/(2sinθ)
= 0.154/(2 × 0.5) nm
= 0.154 nm.

Uses of X-ray diffraction

  • Determination of crystal structure.
  • Measurement of interplanar spacing.
  • Identification of crystalline materials.
  • Study of defects and phases in suitable applications.
Common mistake: In Bragg's law, θ is the glancing/Bragg angle, while many instruments display 2θ. Read the problem carefully before substitution.

4.8 Melting Point Determination of Crystalline Solid by Kjeldahl Method

Important syllabus note: The uploaded curriculum specifically names “melting point determination of crystalline solid by Kjeldahl method.” The syllabus snippet does not provide a complete apparatus diagram or a detailed procedural table. The following is therefore a textbook-level explanation of the principle and a general laboratory description; exact apparatus/procedure should be matched with your institute's practical manual.

Melting point

Definition: The melting point of a crystalline substance is the temperature at which the solid changes into a liquid under specified pressure. A pure crystalline substance generally has a sharp melting point.

Principle

A small quantity of a powdered crystalline sample is heated gradually while its temperature is observed. The temperature at which the solid begins to melt and the temperature at which melting is complete are noted. For a sufficiently pure sample, the melting range is narrow.

General Kjeldahl-type melting point arrangement

Thermometer │ │ ┌──────┴──────┐ │ heating bath │ │ │ │ capillary │ ← sample │ │ │ └─────┴────────┘ heat controlled heating (Exact apparatus arrangement may vary by laboratory manual.)

General procedure

  1. Dry and finely powder the crystalline sample if required by the laboratory instructions.
  2. Fill a small amount of sample into a capillary tube as instructed.
  3. Place the capillary and thermometer in the prescribed heating arrangement.
  4. Heat slowly and uniformly, especially near the expected melting point.
  5. Observe the temperature when melting begins.
  6. Observe the temperature when the sample becomes completely liquid.
  7. Report the melting range and compare it with the expected value where appropriate.

Why slow heating near the melting point?

If the sample is heated too rapidly, the thermometer may not accurately represent the sample temperature and the observed melting range can be misleading.

Observation format:
Sample: __________
Melting begins: __________ °C
Melting complete: __________ °C
Observed melting range: __________ °C

Factors affecting observed melting point

  • Purity of sample
  • Heating rate
  • Particle/sample packing
  • Thermometer calibration
  • Experimental pressure and apparatus conditions
