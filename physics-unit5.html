5.1 PHOTOELECTRIC EFFECT

5.1.1 What is the Photoelectric Effect?

The photoelectric effect is the phenomenon in which electrons are emitted from a material surface when suitable electromagnetic radiation, usually light, falls on it.

LIGHT ↓↓↓↓↓ ┌───────────────────┐ │ METAL SURFACE │ │ • • • • • • • │ → emitted electrons └───────────────────┘

The emitted electrons are called photoelectrons.

5.1.2 Photon

According to the quantum description of light, electromagnetic radiation is made of packets of energy called photons.

E = hν = hc/λ

Here E = photon energy, h = Planck's constant, ν = frequency, c = speed of light and λ = wavelength.

5.1.3 Work Function

The minimum energy required to remove an electron from the surface of a material is called its work function.

φ = hν₀

where φ is work function and ν₀ is threshold frequency.

5.1.4 Threshold Frequency

The minimum frequency of incident radiation required to just produce photoelectric emission from a given material is called the threshold frequency, denoted by ν₀.

If ν < ν₀, photoelectric emission does not occur. If ν ≥ ν₀, emission can occur when other required conditions are satisfied.

5.1.5 Stopping Potential

The minimum reverse potential needed to stop even the fastest photoelectrons from reaching the collector is called stopping potential V₀.

K(max) = eV₀

5.1.6 Einstein's Photoelectric Equation

A photon gives its energy to an electron. Part of this energy is used to overcome the work function and the remaining energy appears as the maximum kinetic energy of the emitted electron.

hν = φ + K(max) hν = hν₀ + ½mv(max)² K(max) = hν − φ = h(ν − ν₀) eV₀ = hν − φ

This equation is one of the most important numerical formulas of this unit.

5.1.7 Step-by-Step Numerical

Example: A metal has threshold frequency 5 × 10¹⁴ Hz. Light of frequency 8 × 10¹⁴ Hz falls on it. Find the maximum kinetic energy of emitted electrons in terms of h.

Using:

K(max) = h(ν − ν₀)

Substitute:

K(max) = h[(8×10¹⁴) − (5×10¹⁴)] = 3×10¹⁴h

So the maximum kinetic energy is 3×10¹⁴h joule.

5.1.8 Important Points

  • Frequency determines photon energy.
  • Threshold frequency depends on the material.
  • Below threshold frequency, increasing intensity alone does not produce photoemission.
  • Stopping potential is related to the maximum kinetic energy.
  • Photoelectric effect supports the quantum nature of light.

5.2 LASERS

5.2.1 Meaning of LASER

LASER stands for Light Amplification by Stimulated Emission of Radiation.

A laser is a source that produces highly directional and coherent electromagnetic radiation through stimulated emission.

5.2.2 Important Properties of Laser Light

PropertyMeaning
MonochromaticLaser light has a very narrow range of wavelengths/frequencies.
CoherentThe emitted waves have a definite phase relationship.
Highly directionalThe beam spreads very little compared with ordinary light.
High intensityLarge optical energy can be concentrated into a small area.

5.2.3 Energy Levels

Atoms have discrete energy levels. An electron can move between levels by absorbing or emitting energy.

Higher energy level E₂ ───────── ↑ absorption / pumping │ ↓ stimulated emission Lower energy level E₁ ─────────
ΔE = E₂ − E₁ = hν

5.2.4 Excitation Potential

The potential required to raise an electron/atom from a lower energy state to a higher excited state is called excitation potential in the context of atomic excitation.

5.2.5 Ionization Potential

The minimum potential corresponding to the energy needed to remove an electron completely from an atom and ionize it is called ionization potential.

5.2.6 Spontaneous Emission

An excited atom can return to a lower energy state on its own and emit a photon. This is called spontaneous emission. The emitted photons are not organized into the same coherent beam required for laser output.

5.2.7 Stimulated Emission

When a photon of suitable energy interacts with an excited atom, it can stimulate the atom to emit another photon. The emitted photon has the same frequency and a definite phase relationship with the stimulating photon. This process is called stimulated emission.

Incoming photon → [EXCITED ATOM] → two matching photons ↓ photon

5.2.8 Population Inversion

Normally, more atoms occupy lower energy states than excited states. For laser action, a condition is created in which the population of the upper laser level is greater than that of the lower relevant level. This is called population inversion.

5.2.9 Pumping

The process of supplying external energy to the active medium to produce population inversion is called pumping.

Depending on the laser system, energy may be supplied by optical or electrical methods. The exact method depends on the type of laser.

5.2.10 Basic Laser Components

Partially reflecting mirror │ ┌────────┴────────┐ │ ACTIVE │ │ MEDIUM │ → Laser output └────────┬────────┘ │ Fully reflecting mirror ↑ Pumping
  • Active medium: material in which laser action occurs.
  • Pumping source: supplies energy.
  • Optical resonator: mirrors provide repeated reflection and help build up stimulated emission.

5.2.11 He-Ne Laser

A He-Ne laser uses a mixture of helium and neon gases as the active medium. Electrical discharge excites the gas system; energy transfer between helium and neon helps establish the required excited-state population, and stimulated emission produces laser radiation.

Exam flow: electrical pumping → excitation → energy transfer → population inversion → stimulated emission → optical amplification → laser output.

5.2.12 p-n Junction Diode Laser

A semiconductor diode laser uses a p-n junction as the active region. When the junction is suitably forward biased, electrons and holes recombine in the active region and photons are produced. Under appropriate conditions, stimulated emission produces laser action.

p-region | active junction region | n-region ← holes electrons → recombination ↓ photon / laser output

AreaExamples

Medicalsurgical procedures, ophthalmic applications and controlled tissue treatment.
Communicationoptical-fiber communication uses laser/optical sources.
Industrialmarking, drilling, cutting and precision processing.

5.3 OPTICAL FIBERS

5.3.1 What is an Optical Fiber?

An optical fiber is a thin, flexible transparent waveguide that carries light from one location to another, mainly by total internal reflection.

5.3.2 Structure

┌───────────────────────────────┐ │ JACKET │ │ ┌───────────────────────┐ │ │ │ CLADDING │ │ │ │ ┌───────────────┐ │ │ │ │ │ CORE │ │ │ │ │ └───────────────┘ │ │ │ └───────────────────────┘ │ └───────────────────────────────┘ Core: light-guiding region Cladding: lower refractive index around core

5.3.3 Total Internal Reflection (TIR)

Total internal reflection occurs when light travels from an optically denser medium to an optically rarer medium, the angle of incidence is greater than the critical angle, and the interface reflects the light back into the denser medium.

For optical fiber operation, the core has a higher refractive index than the cladding.

CORE (higher n) \ / \ \ / \ \ / \ ----\--/--------------\---- boundary CLADDING (lower n)

5.3.4 Acceptance Angle

The maximum angle with the fiber axis at which light can enter the fiber and still be guided through the fiber by total internal reflection is called the acceptance angle.

5.3.5 Numerical Aperture

The numerical aperture (NA) indicates the light-gathering ability of an optical fiber.

NA = n₀ sin θₐ

For air, n₀ ≈ 1, therefore:

NA = sin θₐ

For an ideal step-index fiber in a surrounding medium of refractive index n₀, the common relation is:

NA = √(n₁² − n₂²) / n₀

where n₁ is core refractive index and n₂ is cladding refractive index.

5.3.6 Numerical Example

Example: For a fiber in air, the acceptance angle is 30°. Find NA.
NA = sin θₐ = sin 30° = 0.5

Answer: NA = 0.5.

5.3.7 Types of Optical Fibers

TypeBasic idea
Single-mode fiberDesigned to guide essentially one propagation mode; suitable for long-distance/high-bandwidth communication.
Multimode fiberAllows multiple modes to propagate; commonly used for shorter links and suitable systems.
Step-index fiberCore refractive index is approximately constant and changes abruptly at the core-cladding boundary.
Graded-index fiberCore refractive index changes gradually from center toward the cladding.

5.3.8 Applications

  • Telecommunication: high-speed transmission of voice, data and internet traffic.
  • Medical: endoscopy and delivery/observation of light inside the body in suitable instruments.
  • Sensors: sensing temperature, strain, pressure and other physical quantities in appropriate fiber sensors.
  • Industrial: inspection and communication in environments where electrical wiring may be inconvenient.

5.4 NANOTECHNOLOGY

5.4.1 Meaning

Nanotechnology deals with understanding and controlling materials and structures at the nanometre scale. A nanometre is:

1 nm = 10⁻⁹ m

At very small dimensions, materials can show properties different from their bulk form because surface effects and size-dependent effects become important.

5.4.2 Optical Properties of Nanomaterials

Nanomaterials can interact with light differently from bulk materials. Their optical absorption, scattering, transmission or emission can depend on particle size and structure.

These size-dependent optical properties are useful in areas such as displays, imaging, optical sensors and other photonic applications.

5.4.3 Magnetic Properties

Nanomaterials can exhibit size-dependent magnetic behavior. Magnetic response can change with particle size, composition and structure. Such properties are useful in magnetic storage, sensors, biomedical research and other technologies.

5.4.4 Dielectric Properties

Dielectric properties describe how an insulating material responds to an electric field. At nanoscale dimensions, polarization and interfacial effects can alter dielectric behavior.

These properties are relevant to capacitors, electronic components, sensors and advanced dielectric materials.

5.4.5 Applications of Nanotechnology

FieldExamples
Electronicsminiaturized components, sensors and advanced electronic materials.
Medicineresearch into targeted delivery, imaging and diagnostic technologies.
Energyadvanced electrode, coating and energy-related materials.
Environmentmaterials for sensing, filtration and pollutant treatment research.
Opticsoptical coatings, sensors and size-dependent optical devices.

COMMON MISTAKES

  • Do not confuse threshold frequency with stopping potential.
  • Do not write photon energy as h/ν; the correct relation is hν.
  • Remember that laser action requires stimulated emission and population inversion.
  • In optical fiber, core refractive index must be greater than cladding refractive index for the usual TIR guiding condition.
  • Do not confuse acceptance angle with numerical aperture; they are related but not identical quantities.
  • Do not write 1 nm = 10⁻⁶ m. It is 10⁻⁹ m.
  • For Bhasma, stay within the syllabus statement and do not add unsupported scientific claims in an exam answer.
