1. Nature of Partnership

What is a business?

A business is an activity in which people sell goods or provide services. A business usually aims to earn profit. Examples include a grocery shop, stationery shop, repair service or coaching centre.

What is partnership?

Partnership is the relation between persons who have agreed to share the profits of a business carried on by all, or any of them acting for all. This is the definition in Section 4 of the Indian Partnership Act, 1932.

Easy meaning: Two or more people agree to run a lawful business and share profits. A partner may act on behalf of the firm within the scope of their authority.

Main features

  • Agreement: Partners agree to run a business together.
  • Lawful business: The business must be legal.
  • Profit sharing: Partners agree to share profits.
  • Mutual agency: A partner can act for the firm within their authority.
  • Unlimited liability: In an ordinary partnership, partners may be personally liable for firm debts, subject to applicable law.
  • Two or more persons: At least two persons are required.

Example: Prince invests ₹40,000 and Rahul invests ₹60,000. They open a stationery shop and agree to share profit in the ratio 2:3. They are partners in the firm.

2. Partnership Deed and No-Deed Rules

A partnership deed is a written document containing the terms agreed upon by partners. It helps prevent disputes and guides the preparation of accounts.

Common contents of a deed

  • Name and address of the firm and partners
  • Capital contributed by each partner
  • Profit-sharing ratio
  • Interest on capital and drawings, if agreed
  • Partner salary or commission, if agreed
  • Rules for admission, retirement, death and dissolution

When there is no partnership deed

MatterDefault rule commonly taught
Profit and loss sharingEqually
Interest on capitalNot allowed by default
Interest on drawingsNot charged by default
Partner salary or commissionNot allowed by default
Partner loan to firmGenerally 6% per annum under Section 13(d) of the Indian Partnership Act, 1932
Example: Prince invests ₹80,000 and Rahul invests ₹20,000. If there is no deed, profits are shared equally by default—not in the capital ratio of 4:1.

3. Special Aspects of Partnership Accounts

The Profit and Loss Account calculates the net profit or loss of the business. The Profit and Loss Appropriation Account shows how the net profit is dealt with under the partnership agreement.

Net Profit = Revenue − Expenses

Example: revenue ₹1,00,000 less expenses ₹70,000 gives net profit of ₹30,000.

The Appropriation Account may show interest on capital, partner salary or commission, interest on drawings and the remaining profit distributed to partners, depending on the agreement and question.

4. Partners’ Capital Accounts

Fixed capital method

Two accounts are maintained for each partner: a Capital Account and a Current Account. The Capital Account usually records permanent capital introduced or withdrawn. Routine adjustments such as drawings, interest, salary and profit share generally go to the Current Account.

Fluctuating capital method

One Capital Account is maintained for each partner. Capital introduced, drawings, interest, salary and profit or loss share are recorded in it, so the balance changes.

5. Distribution of Profit

The profit-sharing ratio tells how divisible profit is divided. Add all ratio parts first.

Example: Prince and Rahul share profit 3:2. Divisible profit is ₹50,000.

Total parts = 3 + 2 = 5
Prince = ₹50,000 × 3/5 = ₹30,000
Rahul = ₹50,000 × 2/5 = ₹20,000

Check: ₹30,000 + ₹20,000 = ₹50,000.

Common mistake: Do not divide by one partner’s ratio part alone. Divide by the total ratio parts. Do not assume the profit ratio is the same as the capital ratio.

6. Interest on Capital

Interest on capital is an amount allowed to a partner for capital invested in the firm, if the agreement provides for it.

Interest = Capital × Annual Rate × Time ÷ 100
For months: Interest = Capital × Rate × Months ÷ 1,200

Example A: Full year

Capital ₹80,000 at 10% per annum for one year.

₹80,000 × 10 ÷ 100 = ₹8,000

Example B: Six months

Capital ₹60,000 at 12% per annum for six months.

₹60,000 × 12 × 6 ÷ 1,200 = ₹3,600

Example C: Additional capital

A partner has ₹50,000 from 1 April and adds ₹20,000 on 1 October. Year ends 31 March; rate 10%.

₹50,000 × 10 × 12 ÷ 1,200 = ₹5,000
₹20,000 × 10 × 6 ÷ 1,200 = ₹1,000
Total interest = ₹6,000
Interest on capital is not automatic. Check the deed and question. If profit is insufficient, treatment depends on the agreement and the rule being tested.

7. Changes in Capital and Drawings

Additional capital: If a partner introduces ₹10,000 more, the firm’s bank balance and that partner’s capital increase.

Bank A/c Dr. ₹10,000
 To Partner’s Capital A/c ₹10,000

Drawings: Cash or goods taken by a partner for personal use. Drawings are not a business expense.

Drawings A/c Dr. ₹2,000
 To Bank A/c ₹2,000

Under the fluctuating capital method, drawings are generally closed to Capital Account at the end of the period. Under fixed capital, they are generally recorded through Current Account.

The date matters when calculating interest. Money withdrawn at the beginning of the year is outstanding for longer than money withdrawn at the end.

8. Interest on Drawings

Interest on drawings is charged to a partner for personal withdrawals when the agreement allows it.

Interest = Drawing × Annual Rate × Time ÷ 100

Single drawing

A partner withdraws ₹12,000 at the beginning of the year. Rate = 10%.

₹12,000 × 10 ÷ 100 = ₹1,200

Equal monthly drawings at the beginning of each month

A partner withdraws ₹2,000 at the beginning of each month from April to March. Rate = 12%. Months outstanding are 12, 11, 10, …, 1. Their sum is 78.

Interest = ₹2,000 × 12 × 78 ÷ 1,200 = ₹1,560
If drawings are made at the end of each month, the time period changes. Read dates carefully.

9. Guarantee of Profit to a Partner

A guarantee means a partner is promised a minimum amount of profit. If their normal share is less, the deficiency is borne by the person or persons specified in the agreement.

Example: Prince and Rahul share profit 3:2. Divisible profit is ₹20,000. Rahul guarantees Prince at least ₹14,000.

Prince’s normal share = ₹20,000 × 3/5 = ₹12,000
Rahul’s normal share = ₹20,000 × 2/5 = ₹8,000
Deficiency = ₹14,000 − ₹12,000 = ₹2,000

If the firm or more than one partner gives the guarantee, follow the exact agreement and question.

10. Past Adjustments

Past adjustments correct an earlier profit distribution when an item was omitted or calculated incorrectly—for example, omitted interest on capital, omitted interest on drawings, omitted salary, or an incorrect ratio.

Method

  1. Calculate what each partner should have received.
  2. Calculate what each partner actually received.
  3. Find the difference for each partner.
  4. Identify who must receive and who must give up value.
  5. Pass the net adjustment entry if required.

Example: Prince’s capital is ₹50,000 and Rahul’s is ₹30,000. Interest at 10% was omitted. Interest due is ₹5,000 and ₹3,000. These amounts alone do not determine the final adjustment entry; the original profit distribution and agreement are also needed.

Do not pass an adjustment entry using only the total omitted interest. Compare each partner’s correct entitlement with what was actually credited.

Quick Revision

  • Partnership is based on an agreement to run a lawful business and share profits.
  • A deed records the partners’ terms.
  • Without a deed, profits and losses are generally shared equally; interest on capital, drawings and partner salary are not allowed by default.
  • Fixed capital uses Capital and Current Accounts; fluctuating capital uses one Capital Account.
  • Profit and Loss Account calculates net profit; Appropriation Account deals with distribution.
  • For profit share, divide by total ratio parts.
  • Interest calculations depend on amount, annual rate and time.
  • Guarantee deficiency is the shortfall between guaranteed profit and normal share.
  • Past adjustments compare correct entitlement with actual entitlement.
